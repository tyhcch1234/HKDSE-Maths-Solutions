Probability — Choosing Cans from a Bag

Topic 14: Probability · Multiple-choice · Guided, visual, interactive solution

Probability Combinations \(C^n_r\) Complement Rule "At most" wording

Q The Question

There are 9 cans of apple juice and 4 cans of grape juice in a bag. If 6 cans are randomly chosen from the bag at the same time, find the probability that at most 3 cans of grape juice are chosen.

A \(\dfrac{9}{13}\)
B \(\dfrac{133}{143}\)
C \(\dfrac{140}{143}\)
D \(\dfrac{714}{715}\)

2 Step-by-Step Solution

1 Understand the wording: "at most 3 grape cans" ›

"At most 3" means the number of grape cans chosen is 0, 1, 2 or 3.

Since we pick 6 cans out of 13, the grape count could only ever be 0 to 4 (there are only 4 grape cans). So the only case that is NOT "at most 3" is:

\[ \text{number of grape cans} = 4 \]
Key idea — Complement Rule: \[ P(\text{event}) = 1 - P(\text{event does NOT happen}) \] Here, "at most 3 grape" = everything except "exactly 4 grape".
2 Choose the right formula: counting combinations ›

The cans are chosen "at the same time" — order does not matter. So we use combinations:

\[ C^n_r = \binom{n}{r} = \frac{n!}{r!\,(n-r)!} \]
Meaning of each symbol:
\(n\) = total items to choose from · \(r\) = how many we choose.
\(C^n_r\) counts the number of ways to pick \(r\) items from \(n\) items.

Probability for equally likely outcomes:

\[ P = \frac{\text{number of favourable ways}}{\text{total number of ways}} \]
3 Count the total number of ways to pick 6 cans ›

Total cans \(= 9 + 4 = 13\). We choose 6 of them.

\[ \text{Total ways} = C^{13}_6 \]

Compute it:

\[ C^{13}_6 = \frac{13!}{6!\,7!} = 1716 \]

So there are 1716 equally likely 6-can selections.

4 Count the "bad" case: exactly 4 grape cans ›

If all 4 grape cans are chosen, then the other \(6-4 = 2\) cans must be apple.

Ways to choose the 4 grape cans: \(C^{4}_4\)

Ways to choose the 2 apple cans from 9: \(C^{9}_2\)

\[ \text{Ways} = C^{4}_4 \times C^{9}_2 \]

Since \(C^{4}_4 = 1\), this is just the number of ways to choose the 2 apple cans.

\[ C^{4}_4 \times C^{9}_2 = 1 \times \frac{9!}{2!\,7!} = 36 \]
Why multiply? Choosing the grape cans and choosing the apple cans are independent parts of the same selection — so we multiply the counts (multiplication principle).
5 Apply the complement rule ›

Only 36 of the 1716 selections have 4 grape cans. So everything else satisfies "at most 3 grape".

\[ P(\text{at most 3 grape}) = 1 - P(\text{4 grape cans}) \] \[ = 1 - \frac{C^{4}_4 \, C^{9}_2}{C^{13}_6} \]
Numerator \(C^{4}_4 C^{9}_2\) = favourable ways for "exactly 4 grape".
Denominator \(C^{13}_6\) = total ways. Both are counts, so the ratio is a probability.
6 Simplify to get the answer ›
\[ = 1 - \frac{36}{1716} = 1 - \frac{3}{143} = \frac{143-3}{143} = \frac{140}{143} \]

Check: \(143 \times 12 = 1716\), so \(\frac{36}{1716} = \frac{3}{143}\). ✓

Therefore the answer is C. \(\dfrac{140}{143} \approx 0.979\).

Correct Answer
C · \(\dfrac{140}{143}\)
  • Common trap: reading "at most 3" as "exactly 3". Then you get \(\frac{336}{1716}\) — not an option.
  • Common trap: using permutations \(P^n_r\) instead of combinations. Cans are chosen simultaneously, so order does not matter — use \(C^n_r\).
  • Fastest route: count only the single excluded case (all 4 grape cans) and use \(1-P\).
