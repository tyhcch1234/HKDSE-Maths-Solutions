Functions & Graphs — Recognising transformations of a graph

Function Transformations Reflection Translation Graph Recognition
The Question

Which of the following may represent the graph of \(y=f(x)\) and the graph of \(y=1-f(x)\) on the same rectangular coordinate system?

The options A, B, C, D each show a pair of curves — one is \(y=f(x)\) and the other is claimed to be \(y=1-f(x)\). Only one option is geometrically possible. We shall decide by transforming the graph of \(y=f(x)\) step by step.

Part (a) — The transformation \(y=f(x)\;\longrightarrow\;y=1-f(x)\)

Build the target graph from \(y=f(x)\) in two moves: first reflect, then translate.

Model: \(f(x)=x^2-2x\)
Vertex of \(f\): (1, −1)
Vertex of \(1-f\): (1, 2)
1Reflect in the \(x\)-axis

To obtain \(-f(x)\), every point \((x,\,y)\) on \(y=f(x)\) is sent to \((x,\,-y)\). This reflects the whole curve in the \(x\)-axis.

Hint: Check a key point — if \(f(1)=-1\), then the reflected point is \((1,1)\).
2Translate 1 unit upwards

\[ y=-f(x)\;\xrightarrow{\text{translate up 1}}\;y=-f(x)+1=1-f(x) \]

So the graph of \(y=1-f(x)\) is the reflection of \(y=f(x)\) in the \(x\)-axis, then moved 1 unit up (a vertical shift).

Hint: Vertical shifts preserve the \(x\)-coordinates; only the \(y\)-coordinates change by \(+1\).
3Conclusion

Therefore \(y=1-f(x)\) is the mirror image of \(y=f(x)\) about the \(x\)-axis, raised by \(1\) unit. In particular:

Key relation: \(y=1-f(x)\iff f(x)+y=1\). At each \(x\), the two \(y\)-values sum to \(1\).

This single fact lets us test every option instantly.

Part (b) — Which option is possible?

1Where the companion curve must sit

Since \(y_1=f(x)\) and \(y_2=1-f(x)\) satisfy \(y_1+y_2=1\), the midpoint of the two points at the same \(x\) always lies on the line \(y=\tfrac12\).

Hint: Move the probe slider above and watch the amber midpoint marker stay on the dotted line \(y=\tfrac{1}{2}\).
2Check the orientation

Reflection in the \(x\)-axis turns an upward-opening curve into a downward-opening curve (and vice versa). Then the 1-unit upward shift keeps that orientation.

Fact: If \(y=f(x)\) opens upward, then \(y=1-f(x)\) opens downward, and vice versa.
3Evaluate the options

Options C and D show the companion curve opening the same way as \(y=f(x)\) — impossible after a reflection.

Option B shows the companion curve as a downward parabola sitting entirely below the \(x\)-axis, but a downwards parabola from a reflection lifted by 1 unit must reach above the \(x\)-axis wherever \(f(x)<0\). For example, at the vertex \(x=1\), \(f(1)=-1\) gives \(1-f(1)=2>0\) — the companion curve must have a point with \(y=2\), high above the \(x\)-axis. So B fails.

Answer: A — the companion curve is the exact mirror of \(y=f(x)\) in the \(x\)-axis, shifted \(1\) unit up.

Summary — Answers at a glance

  • Transformation chain: \[ y=f(x)\;\xrightarrow{\text{reflect in }x\text{-axis}}\;y=-f(x)\;\xrightarrow{\text{up }1}\;\boxed{y=1-f(x)} \]
  • Master relation: \(\;f(x)+\big(1-f(x)\big)=1\;\) at every \(x\) — the two curves are mirror images about the horizontal line \(y=\tfrac12\).
  • Orientation rule: the companion curve always opens the opposite way to \(y=f(x)\), so options C and D are impossible.
  • Vertical range rule: wherever \(f(x)<0\), the companion curve has \(1-f(x)>1>0\), so it must rise well above the \(x\)-axis — ruling out option B.
  • Correct option: A — the graph of \(y=1-f(x)\) is the reflection of \(y=f(x)\) in the \(x\)-axis, translated \(1\) unit upwards.
