Quadratic Functions & the Nature of Roots

Using the vertex form \( y = a(x-h)^2 + k \) to decide what must be true about the roots
Vertex Form Quadratic Roots Real / Non-real Roots Translation of Graphs Counter-examples

Q34 The Question Multiple Choice

Let \( f(x) \) be a quadratic function. If the coordinates of the vertex of the graph of \( y = f(x) \) are \( (3,\,-4) \), which of the following must be true?

  1. The roots of the equation \( f(x)=0 \) are integers.
  2. The roots of the equation \( f(x)-3=0 \) are rational numbers.
  3. The roots of the equation \( f(x)+4=0 \) are real numbers.
  4. The roots of the equation \( f(x)+5=0 \) are nonreal numbers.

Part A Set up the vertex form \( f(x)=a(x-3)^2-4 \)

A quadratic whose vertex is \( (3,-4) \) can always be written in vertex form, where \( a \neq 0 \) controls the width and the direction of opening.

Vertex: (3, −4) a = 1 f(x) = 1(x−3)² − 4
Step 11M

The vertex of \( y=f(x) \) is \( (3,-4) \), so the completed-square (vertex) form is:

\[ f(x) = a(x-3)^2 - 4, \qquad a \neq 0 . \]
Hint: the vertex form immediately reveals the turning point: the bracket \((x-3)^2\) is smallest (zero) at \(x=3\), giving the minimum/maximum value \(-4\).
Step 21A

Expand to standard form for later use:

\[ f(x) = ax^2 - 6ax + (9a-4). \]

The discriminant and root-type questions can now be handled uniformly.

\(\boxed{\,f(x)=a(x-3)^2-4\,}\) with \(a\neq 0\)  ⇒  \(\boxed{\,\text{vertex}=(3,-4)\,}\)

Part B Test option A — are \( f(x)=0 \) roots always integers?

Solve \( a(x-3)^2-4=0 \). The roots depend on \(a\). The slider lets you vary \(a\) and watch the parabola cross (or miss) the \(x\)-axis.

a = 1 Δ = 16a roots: 1, 5
Step 11M

Set \( f(x)=0 \):

\[ a(x-3)^2 = 4 \;\Longrightarrow\; (x-3)^2 = \frac{4}{a} \;\Longrightarrow\; x = 3 \pm \sqrt{\frac{4}{a}} . \]
Hint: the roots are symmetric about \(x=3\), the vertex line. Their values clearly depend on \(a\), so nothing about “integers” is forced.
Step 21A

Choose \( a = 2 \): then \( x = 3 \pm \sqrt{2} \), which are irrational — not integers. So option A fails.

Counter-example: \(a=2\) gives \(f(x)=0\) with roots \(3\pm\sqrt2\). Hence A is not always true.
Option A is FALSE — roots need not be integers.

Part C Test option B — are the roots of \( f(x)-3=0 \) always rational?

Here we solve \( a(x-3)^2-4 = 3 \), i.e. \( a(x-3)^2 = 7 \). Drag the slider to see how the horizontal line \( y=3 \) meets the parabola.

a = 1 horizontal line: y = 3 roots: 3 ± √7
Step 11M

Solve \( f(x)-3=0 \) i.e. \( f(x)=3 \):

\[ a(x-3)^2 - 4 = 3 \;\Longrightarrow\; a(x-3)^2 = 7 \;\Longrightarrow\; x = 3 \pm \sqrt{\frac{7}{a}} . \]
Step 21A

Take \( a=1 \): the roots are \( 3 \pm \sqrt{7} \), which are irrational, hence not rational. So option B fails.

Counter-example: \(a=1\) gives roots \(3\pm\sqrt7 \notin \mathbb{Q}\).
Option B is FALSE — roots need not be rational.

Part D Prove option C is the answer

Solve \( f(x)+4 = 0 \), i.e. \( f(x) = -4 \). The horizontal line \( y=-4 \) just touches the vertex of the parabola — the key geometric insight.

Line: y = −4 Double root: x = 3 Independent of a!
Step 11M

Set \( f(x)+4 = 0 \):

\[ a(x-3)^2 - 4 + 4 = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; a(x-3)^2 = 0 . \]
Step 21A

Since \( a \neq 0 \), divide both sides:

\[ (x-3)^2 = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; x = 3 \quad (\text{a repeated root}). \]

This root is real for every admissible value of \(a\).

Why? The line \(y=-4\) is exactly the tangent at the vertex, so the parabola always meets it at the single point \((3,-4)\).
Option C is TRUE — for every \(a\neq0\), \(f(x)+4=0\) has the real root \(x=3\).

Part E Show option D can fail

Solve \( f(x)+5 = 0 \), i.e. \( f(x) = -5 \). The line \( y=-5 \) is below the vertex, so the outcome depends on the sign of \(a\).

Line: y = −5 a = 1 no real intersection
Step 11M

Set \( f(x)+5 = 0 \):

\[ a(x-3)^2 - 4 + 5 = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; a(x-3)^2 = -1 \;\Longrightarrow\; (x-3)^2 = -\frac{1}{a}. \]
Step 21A

The right-hand side is negative when \(a>0\), so there are no real roots then. But when \( a = -1 \) it becomes

\[ (x-3)^2 = 1 \;\Longrightarrow\; x = 3 \pm 1 = 2 \text{ or } 4 , \]

which are real numbers. So option D fails.

Counter-example: \(a=-1\) gives the real roots \(2\) and \(4\).
Option D is FALSE — roots can be real.

Summary — Answer at a Glance

  • Vertex form: \( f(x)=a(x-3)^2-4 \), \( a\neq 0 \).
  • A: \( f(x)=0 \Rightarrow x = 3\pm\sqrt{4/a} \) — not always integers. ✗
  • B: \( f(x)-3=0 \Rightarrow x = 3\pm\sqrt{7/a} \) — not always rational. ✗
  • C: \( f(x)+4=0 \Rightarrow (x-3)^2 = 0 \Rightarrow x=3 \) — always real. ✓
  • D: \( f(x)+5=0 \Rightarrow (x-3)^2 = -1/a \) — can be real, e.g. \(a=-1\). ✗
  • Correct choice: C.
