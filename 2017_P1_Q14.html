Question

Let $f(x)=6x^{3}-13x^{2}-46x+34$. When $f(x)$ is divided by $2x^{2}+ax+4$, the quotient and the remainder are $3x+7$ and $bx+c$ respectively, where $a$, $b$ and $c$ are constants.

(a) Find $a$. [3 marks]

(b) Let $g(x)$ be a quadratic polynomial such that when $g(x)$ is divided by $2x^{2}+ax+4$, the remainder is $bx+c$.

(i) Prove that $f(x)-g(x)$ is divisible by $2x^{2}+ax+4$.

(ii) Someone claims that all the roots of the equation $f(x)-g(x)=0$ are integers. Do you agree? Explain your answer. [5 marks]

Step-by-step Solution

Step 1 · Part (a)

Write the division identity. Dividing $f(x)$ by $2x^{2}+ax+4$ gives quotient $3x+7$ and remainder $bx+c$, so

$$f(x)=(2x^{2}+ax+4)(3x+7)+bx+c.$$

Expand the product:

$$\begin{aligned}(2x^{2}+ax+4)(3x+7)&=6x^{3}+14x^{2}+3ax^{2}+7ax+12x+28\\&=6x^{3}+(3a+14)x^{2}+(7a+12)x+28.\end{aligned}$$

Adding $bx+c$:

$$f(x)=6x^{3}+(3a+14)x^{2}+(7a+12+b)x+(28+c).$$
Hint: A division can always be rewritten as “divisor × quotient + remainder”.
Division Identity for Polynomials \(f(x)=D(x)Q(x)+R(x)\) Dividing by \(2x^{2}+ax+4\) with quotient \(3x+7\) and remainder \(bx+c\) gives this identity for \(f\).
Step 2 · Compare coefficients

But we are also given $f(x)=6x^{3}-13x^{2}-46x+34$. Equating the $x^{2}$ coefficients:

$$3a+14=-13.$$

Solving: $3a=-27$, so

$$\boxed{a=-9}.$$

With $a=-9$ one also gets, by comparing the $x$ and constant terms, $7(-9)+12+b=-46 \Rightarrow b=5$ and $28+c=34 \Rightarrow c=6$.

Hint: Only the $x^{2}$ term is needed to find $a$ — that is why it is worth 1M + 1M + 1A.
Comparing Coefficients \(\text{equal polynomials have equal coefficients of each power}\) The \(x^{2}\) coefficient on the left is \(3a+14\) and on the right \(-13\), so \(3a+14=-13\).
Step 3 · Part (b)(i)

Since $g(x)$ divided by $2x^{2}+ax+4$ leaves the same remainder $bx+c$, we can write

$$g(x)=k(2x^{2}+ax+4)+bx+c$$

for some constant $k$ (the quotient of $g$, which is a constant because $g$ is quadratic).

Subtract from $f(x)=(2x^{2}+ax+4)(3x+7)+bx+c$:

$$\begin{aligned}f(x)-g(x)&=(2x^{2}+ax+4)(3x+7)+bx+c-\big[k(2x^{2}+ax+4)+bx+c\big]\\&=(2x^{2}+ax+4)(3x+7)-k(2x^{2}+ax+4)\\&=(2x^{2}+ax+4)(3x+7-k).\end{aligned}$$

Hence $f(x)-g(x)$ is divisible by $2x^{2}+ax+4$. $\blacksquare$

Hint: subtracting the common remainder kills the $bx+c$ terms, leaving a common factor.
Common Factor Law \(AB-AC=A(B-C)\) After subtracting, \(2x^{2}+ax+4\) is a common factor of both terms, leaving \(3x+7-k\).
Step 4 · Part (b)(ii) — Factorise

Using $a=-9$ and $b=5,\ c=6$:

$$f(x)-g(x)=(2x^{2}-9x+4)(3x+7-k)=0.$$

Factorise the quadratic factor:

$$2x^{2}-9x+4=(2x-1)(x-4).$$

So the equation becomes

$$(2x-1)(x-4)(3x+7-k)=0.$$
Hint: $2x\cdot x=2x^2$ and $(-1)\cdot(-4)=4$ check out; the middle term $-1\cdot x-4\cdot 2x=-9x$.
Step 5 · Conclusion

The roots of $f(x)-g(x)=0$ include the roots of $2x^{2}-9x+4=0$, namely

$$x=\tfrac{1}{2}\quad\text{and}\quad x=4.$$

Since $\tfrac{1}{2}$ is not an integer, the claim that all roots are integers is false.

$\therefore$ Disagree — not all roots of $f(x)-g(x)=0$ are integers.

Hint: a single non-integer root is enough to disprove the universal claim.
ANSWER (a) $a=-9$  ·  (b)(i) $f(x)-g(x)=(2x^{2}+ax+4)(3x+7-k)$, hence divisible  ·  (b)(ii) Disagree: $x=\tfrac12$ is a root, which is not an integer.
