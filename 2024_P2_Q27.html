QQuestion

Denote the circle \(3x^{2}+3y^{2}-6x+12y-4=0\) by \(C\).

Which of the following are true?

I. The origin lies inside \(C\).
II. The circumference of \(C\) is less than \(16\).
III. The perpendicular distance from the centre of \(C\) to the \(x\)-axis is \(2\).

A I and II only
B I and III only
C II and III only
D I, II and III

Correct option: D — all three statements are true.

IStatement I — Does the origin lie inside \(C\)?

Move the slider to slide the point \(P\) and compare \(PG\) with the radius \(r\).

Centre \(G = (1,\,-2)\)
Radius \(r=\sqrt{19/3}\approx 2.5166\)
\(OG=\sqrt{a^2+b^2}=\sqrt{0.00} = 0.00\)
\(OG < r\) → inside
Step 1Simplification

Divide the whole equation by \(3\) to get the standard form:

\[ 3x^{2}+3y^{2}-6x+12y-4=0 \] \[ \Rightarrow\; x^{2}+y^{2}-2x+4y-\frac{4}{3}=0 \]
Hint: A circle in general form \(x^{2}+y^{2}+Dx+Ey+F=0\) must have equal coefficients of \(x^{2}\) and \(y^{2}\) — divide them to 1 first.
Step 2Centre & Radius

Compare with \(x^{2}+y^{2}+Dx+Ey+F=0\): here \(D=-2,\;E=4,\;F=-\frac{4}{3}\).

Centre \(G=\left(-\dfrac{D}{2},\,-\dfrac{E}{2}\right)=\left(\dfrac{-(-2)}{2},\,\dfrac{-4}{2}\right)=(1,\,-2)\).

Radius \(r=\sqrt{\left(\dfrac{D}{2}\right)^{2}+\left(\dfrac{E}{2}\right)^{2}-F}=\sqrt{(1)^{2}+(-2)^{2}-\left(-\dfrac{4}{3}\right)}=\sqrt{\dfrac{19}{3}}\).

Hint: Centre \(=(-D/2,\,-E/2)\) and \(r=\sqrt{(D/2)^2+(E/2)^2-F}\). Watch the sign of \(F\)!
\(G=(1,\,-2)\),   \(r=\sqrt{19/3}\approx2.52\)
Step 3Test the origin

Distance from the origin \(O\) to the centre \(G\):

\[ OG=\sqrt{(1-0)^{2}+(-2-0)^{2}}=\sqrt{1+4}=\sqrt{5}\approx2.236 \]

Compare with the radius:

\[ \sqrt{5}<\sqrt{\frac{19}{3}}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad 5<\frac{19}{3}\approx6.33 \quad\checkmark \]

IIStatement II — Is the circumference less than 16?

Drag the radius slider. The dashed line shows the target circumference \(16\). The circle on the right is the true circle \(C\).

True radius \(r=\sqrt{19/3}\approx 2.5166\)
True circumference \(2\pi r\approx 15.813\)
\(15.813<16\) → true
Step 1Circumference formula

Circumference of a circle of radius \(r\):

\[ C_{\text{circ}}=2\pi r \]
Hint: The units of \(r\) and the circumference must match; here both are in coordinate units.
Step 2Substitute \(r\)

With \(r=\sqrt{\dfrac{19}{3}}\approx2.5166\):

\[ 2\pi r=2\pi\sqrt{\frac{19}{3}}\approx 2\pi(2.5166)\approx 15.813 \]
Hint: Use \(\pi\approx3.14159\). The answer \(15.813\) is uncomfortably close to 16 — so double-check by squaring.
Step 3Exact comparison

Square both sides to compare exactly without approximating:

\[ \left(2\pi\sqrt{\frac{19}{3}}\right)^{2}=4\pi^{2}\cdot\frac{19}{3}=\frac{76\pi^{2}}{3}\approx 250.0 < 256=16^{2} \]

Since both sides are positive, \(2\pi r<16\).

Hint: If you approximate, you may be unsure whether \(15.8<16\); squaring gives a clean comparison.
Statement II is true. \(2\pi r\approx15.81<16\).

IIIStatement III — Distance from the centre to the \(x\)-axis

Toggle the perpendicular foot \(F\). The perpendicular distance from \(G\) to the \(x\)-axis is simply \(|y_G|\).

Centre \(G=(1,\,-2)\)
Perpendicular foot \(F=(1,\,0)\)
\(GF=|y_G|=|-2| = 2\)
\(GF = 2\) → true
Step 1Locate the centre

From Part (I), the centre of \(C\) is

\[ G=(1,\,-2) \]
Hint: The \(x\)-coordinate of \(G\) is irrelevant for a perpendicular distance to the \(x\)-axis.
Step 2Distance formula

The perpendicular from \(G\) to the \(x\)-axis hits the \(x\)-axis at \(F=(1,\,0)\). So

\[ GF=|y_G-0|=|-2-0|=2 \]
Hint: The distance from \((x_0,y_0)\) to the line \(y=0\) is \(|y_0|\) — no computation beyond reading the \(y\)-coordinate.
Step 3Conclude

Since \(GF=2\), statement III is correct.

Statement III is true. \(GF=|{-2}|=2\).
