Percentage Change with a Changing Base

A 4-mark question with a classic trap: the same “20%” means two different things, because the two percentages are measured against different base amounts.

Question

The daily wage of Ada is 20% higher than that of Billy while the daily wage of Billy is 20% lower than that of Christine. It is given that the daily wage of Billy is $480.

(a) Find the daily wage of Ada.

(b) Who has the highest daily wage? Explain your answer. [4 marks]

Same percentage, different base! The percentage increase uses Billy’s wage as the base, while the percentage decrease uses Christine’s (larger) wage as the base. So the two “20%” are not equal in dollars.

Let the base (Billy’s wage) be \(B\) and the common percentage be \(r\) (as a decimal).

\[ \text{Ada} = B(1+r), \qquad \text{Christine} = \frac{B}{1-r} \] \[ \frac{B}{1-r} - B(1+r) = \frac{B - B(1-r)(1+r)}{1-r} = \frac{Br^{2}}{1-r} \]

Since \(0 < r < 1\), this difference is always positive — so Christine’s wage is always higher than Ada’s, no matter which percentage you choose.

Step-by-Step Solution

Reveal one step at a time. Try to predict each line before you click.

1 Part (a) — Identify the base amount

“Ada’s wage is 20% higher than Billy’s” — the comparison is made against Billy’s wage. So Billy’s wage is the base (treated as 100%).

\[ \text{Ada's wage} = \text{Billy's wage} \times (1 + 20\%) \]
Rule: “\(A\) is \(p\%\) higher than \(B\)” means \(A = B \times (1 + \tfrac{p}{100})\). The word “than” tells you which quantity is the base — here it is Billy, whose wage we already know ($480).
2 Part (a) — Compute Ada’s wage

Substitute Billy’s wage ($480) into the formula:

\[ \text{Ada's wage} = 480 \times (1 + 20\%) = 480 \times 1.2 = \$576 \]

So Ada’s daily wage is $576. 1M method1A answer

\(20\% = 0.2\), so \(1 + 20\% = 1.2\). Multiplying by 1.2 increases the amount by 20% in a single step — no need to find “20% of 480” separately.
3 Part (b) — Spot the trap: the base has changed

“Billy’s wage is 20% lower than Christine’s.” This time the base is Christine’s wage, which is unknown.

A common mistake is to write \(480 \times 1.2\) or \(480 \times 0.8\) — both are wrong. The 20% must be taken from Christine’s wage, not from Billy’s.

Reverse percentage: “Billy is 20% lower than Christine” gives \[ \text{Billy} = \text{Christine} \times (1 - 20\%). \] The unknown sits inside the bracket, so we form an equation and solve for it.
4 Part (b) — Set up an equation

Let \(\$x\) be the daily wage of Christine. Then:

\[ x(1 - 20\%) = 480 \] \[ 0.8x = 480 \]

1M method

\(1 - 20\% = 0.8\). Christine’s full wage is \(x\); Billy receives only 80% of it, and that 80% equals $480.
5 Part (b) — Solve for Christine’s wage
\[ x = \frac{480}{1 - 20\%} = \frac{480}{0.8} = 600 \]

So Christine’s daily wage is $600.

Always divide when the unknown is the base of a percentage decrease. Check: \(80\%\) of $600 is $480 ✓.
6 Part (b) — Compare and conclude

Line up the three wages:

\[ \text{Christine } \$600 \;>\; \text{Ada } \$576 \;>\; \text{Billy } \$480 \]

Christine has the highest daily wage. 1A answer

The “explain” mark is earned by actually comparing the computed values — a bare “Christine” without working does not get the method mark.

Key Takeaway & Common Mistakes

✅ Remember

  • Percentage change is always measured from a base (“than” introduces the base).
  • Increase: \(\text{New} = \text{Base} \times (1 + p\%)\).
  • Decrease: \(\text{New} = \text{Base} \times (1 - p\%)\).
  • If the unknown is the base, divide instead: \(\text{Base} = \dfrac{\text{New}}{1 \pm p\%}\).

⚠️ Watch out

  • A 20% increase followed by a 20% decrease does not return to the original value.
  • Never apply a percentage decrease to the wrong amount (here, to Billy’s $480).
  • Do not assume “+20% then −20%” cancels out: \(480 \times 1.2 = 576\), but \(480 \div 0.8 = 600\).
