QQuestion

If the first five digits and the last two digits of a seven-digit password are formed by a permutation of 1, 3, 5, 7, 9 and a permutation of 2, 8 respectively, how many different seven-digit passwords can be formed?
  • A10
  • B240
  • C480
  • D5 040

A seven-digit password has 7 positions. The question splits them into two independent blocks:

\[ \underbrace{\_\ \_\ \_\ \_\ \_}_{\text{first 5 digits}}\ \underbrace{\_\ \_}_{\text{last 2 digits}} \]

Block 1 (positions 1–5): must be a permutation (an arrangement, no repeats) of \(\{1,3,5,7,9\}\).
Block 2 (positions 6–7): must be a permutation of \(\{2,8\}\).

HintThe two sets of digits do not overlap (\(1,3,5,7,9\) are all odd, \(2,8\) are even), so the choices in Block 1 have no effect on Block 2. That independence is why we will multiply later.

We must place all 5 of the digits \(1,3,5,7,9\) into 5 boxes, using each exactly once. That is the number of permutations of 5 distinct objects:

\[ {}_5P_5 = 5! = 5 \times 4 \times 3 \times 2 \times 1 = 120 \]
Hint — the "slot" viewSlot 1 has 5 choices → slot 2 has 4 → slot 3 has 3 → slot 4 has 2 → slot 5 has 1. Multiply them: \(5 \times 4 \times 3 \times 2 \times 1 = 120\).

So there are 120 ways to fill the first five positions.

Now place \(2\) and \(8\) into the 2 remaining boxes, each used once:

\[ {}_2P_2 = 2! = 2 \times 1 = 2 \]

The two possibilities are simply \(\;2\,8\;\) and \(\;8\,2\).

For each of the 120 arrangements of the first five digits, there are 2 arrangements of the last two digits. So we multiply:

\[ \text{Total} = 5! \times 2! = 120 \times 2 = 240 \]
Hint — why multiply, not add?Adding would give \(120 + 2 = 122\), which would mean choosing either block. But a complete password needs both blocks filled at the same time — independent consecutive choices are multiplied.

There are 240 different seven-digit passwords.

Answer: B  (240)

Why the other choices are tempting:

  • A (10): just \(5 + 2 +\) … an addition/digit-counting error — e.g. counting the number of digits used: \(5+2=7\), or \(5\times2=10\).
  • C (480): computing \(5! \times 2 = 240\) then doubling — or using \(2^2\) instead of \(2!\).
  • D (5 040): this is \(7! = 5040\), treating all seven digits as freely permutable. But the digits \(2, 8\) are forced into the last two positions, so \(7!\) over-counts badly.
Key takeaway: when a password is split into restricted blocks, count each block separately, then multiply the results.
