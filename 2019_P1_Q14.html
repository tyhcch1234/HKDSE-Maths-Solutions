Geometry — Congruence, Similar Triangles & Comparing Lengths

Congruent Triangles (ASA) Similar Triangles (AAA) Parallel Lines Isosceles Triangle Square & Diagonal

QUESTION

Full question with all parts and marks.

In Figure 2, \(ABCD\) is a square. It is given that \(E\) is a point lying on \(AD\). \(BD\) and \(CE\) intersect at the point \(F\). Let \(G\) be a point such that \(BG \parallel EC\) and \(CG \parallel DB\).

(a) Prove that
(i) \(\triangle BCG \cong \triangle CBF\),   [2]
(ii) \(\triangle BCF \sim \triangle DEF\).   [2]

(b) Suppose that \(\angle BCF = \angle BGC\).
(i) Let \(BC = \ell\). Express \(DF\) in terms of \(\ell\).   [2]
(ii) Someone claims that \(AE > DF\). Do you agree? Explain your answer.   [2]

Total: 8 marks

PART (a)(i) Prove \(\triangle BCG \cong \triangle CBF\)

Use the parallel sides to find alternate angles, then apply ASA.

\(\angle BCG\) = \(\angle CBF\) (alt. \(\angle\)s, \(CG \parallel DB\)) \(\angle CBG\) = \(\angle BCF\) (alt. \(\angle\)s, \(BG \parallel EC\)) \(BC = BC\) (common side)
11M
Since \(CG \parallel DB\), \[\angle BCG = \angle CBF \quad (\text{alt. } \angle\text{s, } CG \parallel DB).\]
Hint: \(DB\) produces the diagonal line through \(B\) and \(D\); the alternate angle pair sits on the transverse \(BC\).
21M
Since \(BG \parallel EC\), \[\angle CBG = \angle BCF \quad (\text{alt. } \angle\text{s, } BG \parallel EC).\]
Hint: The line \(EC\) and the line \(BG\) are parallel, and \(BC\) is the transversal joining them.
31A
Also \(BC\) is a common side to both triangles: \[BC = BC \quad (\text{common side}).\]
Hint: This is the side between the two angle pairs you just found in each triangle — perfect for ASA.
41A
Therefore, \[\triangle BCG \cong \triangle CBF \quad (\text{ASA}).\]
✔ \(\triangle BCG \cong \triangle CBF\) (ASA)

PART (a)(ii) Prove \(\triangle BCF \sim \triangle DEF\)

Three pairs of equal angles give similarity (AAA).

\(\angle CBF = \angle EDF\) (alt. \(\angle\)s, \(BC \parallel ED\)) \(\angle BFC = \angle DFE\) (vert. opp. \(\angle\)s) \(\angle BCF = \angle DEF\) (\(\angle\) sum of \(\triangle\))
11M
Since \(ABCD\) is a square, \(BC \parallel AD\), i.e. \(BC \parallel ED\). Hence \[\angle CBF = \angle EDF \quad (\text{alt. } \angle\text{s, } BC \parallel ED).\]
Hint: \(BD\) is the transversal; look at the angles it makes with the two parallel sides \(BC\) and \(ED\).
21M
At the intersection point \(F\), \[\angle BFC = \angle DFE \quad (\text{vert. opp. } \angle\text{s}).\]
Hint: \(BD\) and \(CE\) cross at \(F\), giving a pair of vertically opposite angles.
31A
The remaining pair of angles must then be equal: \[\angle BCF = \angle DEF \quad (\angle \text{ sum of } \triangle).\]
Hint: Angle sum of a triangle is \(180^\circ\); subtract the two known equal angles in each triangle.
41A
All three pairs of corresponding angles of the two triangles are equal, so \[\triangle BCF \sim \triangle DEF \quad (\text{AAA}).\]
✔ \(\triangle BCF \sim \triangle DEF\) (AAA)

PART (b)(i) Express \(DF\) in terms of \(\ell\)

Use the congruency from (a)(i) to get an isosceles triangle, then use the diagonal \(BD = \sqrt{2}\,\ell\).

\(BC = BF = \ell\) \(BD = \sqrt{2}\,\ell\) \(DF = BD - BF = (\sqrt{2}-1)\ell\)
11M
From (a)(i), \(\triangle BCG \cong \triangle CBF\), so \[\angle BGC = \angle BFC.\] The question gives \(\angle BCF = \angle BGC\). Combining, \[\angle BCF = \angle BFC.\]
Hint: Congruent triangles give equal corresponding angles; then the extra given condition transfers the equality onto \(\angle BFC\).
21A
Since \(\angle BCF = \angle BFC\), \(\triangle BCF\) is isosceles with \[BF = BC = \ell.\]
Hint: Equal base angles \(\Rightarrow\) equal opposite sides. The base angle at \(F\) faces \(BC\), and the base angle at \(C\) faces \(BF\).
31M
In the square, \(BD\) is a diagonal. Using the \(45^\circ\) angle in \(\triangle BCD\), \[BD\cos 45^\circ = BC = \ell \;\Longrightarrow\; BD = \sqrt{2}\,\ell.\]
Hint: \(\cos 45^\circ = \dfrac{BC}{BD}\). Equivalently, a square of side \(\ell\) has diagonal \(\sqrt{2}\,\ell\) (Pythagoras).
41A
Since \(F\) lies on \(BD\), \[DF = BD - BF = \sqrt{2}\,\ell - \ell = (\sqrt{2}-1)\ell.\]
✔ \(DF = (\sqrt{2}-1)\,\ell\)
0 / 4

PART (b)(ii) Is \(AE > DF\)? Explain.

Compare \(AE\) with \((\sqrt{2}-1)\ell\) and also use the fact that \(AE + DE = \ell\).

\(AE = (2-\sqrt{2})\ell \approx 0.5858\,\ell\) \(DF = (\sqrt{2}-1)\ell \approx 0.4142\,\ell\) \(AE - DF = (3-2\sqrt{2})\ell > 0\)
11M
From (b)(i), \(\triangle BCF\) is isosceles with \(BC = BF\). From (a)(ii), \(\triangle BCF \sim \triangle DEF\), and since \(BF = BC\), \(\triangle DEF\) is also isosceles with \[DE = DF = (\sqrt{2}-1)\ell.\]
Hint: Similar triangles preserve the ratio of corresponding sides; if \(BC = BF\) then the corresponding sides \(DE\) and \(DF\) must be equal too.
21A
Since \(ABCD\) is a square of side \(\ell\), \(AD = \ell\). Therefore \[AE = AD - DE = \ell - (\sqrt{2}-1)\ell = (2-\sqrt{2})\ell.\]
Hint: \(E\) lies on \(AD\), so \(AD = AE + ED\). Substitute \(ED = (\sqrt{2}-1)\ell\).
31M
Compare \(AE\) with \(\dfrac{\ell}{2}\): \[AE = (2-\sqrt{2})\ell > \left(2-\tfrac{3}{2}\right)\ell = \frac{\ell}{2}.\] Note \(AE + DE = \ell\), so \(DE < \dfrac{\ell}{2}\), and since \(DE = DF\), we get \(DF < \dfrac{\ell}{2}\).
Hint: \(\sqrt{2} \approx 1.414 < 1.5 = \dfrac{3}{2}\). This lets us bound \(AE\) below by \(\dfrac{\ell}{2}\), while \(DF\) stays below it.
41A
Since \(AE > \dfrac{\ell}{2} > DF\), \[AE > DF.\]
✔ Yes, the claim is agreed: \(AE > DF\). (Numerically \(AE \approx 0.586\,\ell\), \(DF \approx 0.414\,\ell\).)
0 / 4

SUMMARY Final Answers at a Glance

  • (a)(i) \(\triangle BCG \cong \triangle CBF\) — because \(BC = BC\) (common), \(\angle BCG = \angle CBF\) (alt. \(\angle\)s, \(CG \parallel DB\)), \(\angle CBG = \angle BCF\) (alt. \(\angle\)s, \(BG \parallel EC\)); hence by ASA.
  • (a)(ii) \(\triangle BCF \sim \triangle DEF\) — because \(\angle CBF = \angle EDF\) (alt. \(\angle\)s, \(BC \parallel ED\)), \(\angle BFC = \angle DFE\) (vert. opp. \(\angle\)s), and \(\angle BCF = \angle DEF\) (\(\angle\) sum of \(\triangle\)); hence by AAA.
  • (b)(i) \(DF = (\sqrt{2}-1)\,\ell\).
  • (b)(ii) Yes, \(AE > DF\). In fact \(AE = (2-\sqrt{2})\ell \approx 0.586\,\ell\), which exceeds \(\dfrac{\ell}{2}\), while \(DF < \dfrac{\ell}{2}\).
