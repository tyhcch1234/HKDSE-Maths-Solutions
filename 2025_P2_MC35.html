Complex Numbers: Sum of Powers of $i$

An interactive, step-by-step walkthrough of the geometric-series method.

Complex Numbers Powers of i Geometric Series Rationalising Denominators

Question

$$i^{9}+i^{10}+i^{11}+\dots+i^{999}$$
A. $-1$
B. $0$
C. $1$
D. $i$

Key Idea & Learning Highlight

The sum is a geometric series with first term $a=i^{9}$ and common ratio $r=i$. Powers of $i$ repeat with period 4: $$i,\;-1,\;-i,\;1,\;i,\;-1,\;-i,\;1,\;\dots$$

Since $999-9+1=991$ terms (an odd number), a naive "pairing" argument fails — but the geometric-series formula handles it in one line: $$i^{9}+i^{10}+\dots+i^{999}=\frac{i^{9}\left(1-i^{991}\right)}{1-i}$$

Steps revealed: 0 / 6
1 Recognise the structure: a geometric series ▾

Look at consecutive terms. Each term is obtained by multiplying the previous term by $i$:

$$\underbrace{i^{9}}_{\text{first term } a},\quad \underbrace{i^{10}=i^{9}\cdot i}_{},\quad \underbrace{i^{11}=i^{10}\cdot i}_{},\quad \dots,\quad i^{999}$$

So it is a geometric series with

$$a=i^{9},\qquad r=i,\qquad \text{number of terms } n = 999-9+1 = 991$$
Formula (geometric series): $$S_n = \frac{a\left(1-r^{\,n}\right)}{1-r}\quad (r\neq 1)$$ with $a$ = first term, $r$ = common ratio, $n$ = number of terms. Here $a=i^9$, $r=i$, $n=991$.
2 Apply the geometric-series formula ▾

Substitute $a=i^{9}$, $r=i$, $n=991$:

$$i^{9}+i^{10}+i^{11}+\dots+i^{999} =\frac{i^{9}\left(1-i^{991}\right)}{1-i}$$
Check: the exponent in the numerator is $a\cdot r^{\,n} = i^{9}\cdot i^{991} = i^{9+991}=i^{1000}$, which indeed equals the "one term after the last" $i^{999}\cdot i = i^{1000}$. The formula works for any $r\neq 1$ — including the complex number $r=i$.
3 Reduce $i^{9}$ and $i^{991}$ using the 4-cycle ▾

The pattern $i,\,-1,\,-i,\,1$ repeats every 4 powers. Reduce each exponent modulo 4:

$$9 = 4\times 2 + 1 \;\Longrightarrow\; i^{9}=i^{1}=i$$ $$991 = 4\times 247 + 3 \;\Longrightarrow\; i^{991}=i^{3}=-i$$

Substitute these back:

$$=\frac{i\left[1-(-i)\right]}{1-i}=\frac{i(1+i)}{1-i}$$
Powers-of-$i$ rule: for integer $n$, write $n=4q+r$ where $0\le r\le 3$. Then $i^{\,n}=i^{\,r}$, i.e. $$i^{4q}=1,\quad i^{4q+1}=i,\quad i^{4q+2}=-1,\quad i^{4q+3}=-i$$
4 Rationalise the denominator (multiply by the conjugate) ▾

The denominator $1-i$ is complex, so multiply numerator and denominator by the conjugate $1+i$:

$$=\frac{i(1+i)}{1-i}\cdot\frac{1+i}{1+i} =\frac{i(1+i)(1+i)}{(1-i)(1+i)}$$
Why the conjugate? For any complex number $a+bi$, multiplying by its conjugate gives a real number: $$(1-i)(1+i)=1^2-i^2=1-(-1)=1+1=2$$ This removes the imaginary part from the denominator.
5 Expand the numerator and simplify ▾

Expand the numerator, remembering $i^{2}=-1$:

$$i(1+i)(1+i)=i(1+2i+i^{2})=i\bigl(1+2i+(-1)\bigr)=i(2i)=2i^{2}=-2$$

And the denominator is $1+1=2$. So:

$$=\frac{i\left(1+2i+i^{2}\right)}{1+1}=\frac{-2}{2}=-1$$
Common slip: students often forget $i^{2}=-1$. Keeping $i^{2}=-1$ in the expansion is what turns the imaginary expression into a real number at the end.
6 Final answer ▾
$$i^{9}+i^{10}+i^{11}+\dots+i^{999}=-1$$

Therefore the correct option is:

A. $-\,1$
