Coordinate Geometry — mid-point of the chord cut off by a line on a circle
Line & Circle Intersection Sum of Roots Mid-point Formula Multiple Choice

Q The Question MC

The straight line \(2x - y - 6 = 0\) and the circle \[x^{2} + y^{2} - 8y - 14 = 0\] intersect at \(P\) and \(Q\). Find the \(y\)-coordinate of the mid-point of \(PQ\).

A. \(-4\)
B. \(-2\)
C. \(2\)
D. \(4\)

Answer: C — revealed at the end of the exploration below.

1 Set up the picture: line meets circle

Before any algebra, let us look at what is actually happening. The line cuts the circle at two points \(P\) and \(Q\). Drag the sliders to move the line, and see the two intersection points and their mid-point move.

iCircle in standard form

Complete the square on \(y\): \[x^{2}+y^{2}-8y-14=0 \;\Longrightarrow\; x^{2}+(y-4)^{2}=14+16=30.\] So the centre is \(C(0,4)\) and the radius is \(r=\sqrt{30}\approx 5.48\).

iiLine in slope–intercept form

The line \(2x-y-6=0\) can be written \(y=2x-6\), i.e. slope \(m=2\) and \(y\)-intercept \(-6\).

Hint. Changing \(c\) changes the vertical position of the line, but never its slope — so the mid-point always slides along a fixed direction.

2 Substitute and form a quadratic in \(y\)

Make \(x\) the subject of the line, then substitute into the circle. Watch the substitution happen live as you drag the slider.

From \(2x-y-6=0\) we get \(x=\dfrac{y+6}{2}\). Substituting into the circle:

\[\left(\frac{y+6}{2}\right)^{2}+y^{2}-8y-14=0\] \[\frac{(y+6)^{2}}{4}+y^{2}-8y-14=0\] \[(y+6)^{2}+4y^{2}-32y-56=0\] \[5y^{2}-20y-20=0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad y^{2}-4y-4=0 \qquad (\ast)\] 0 / 2
1Small numbers, same roots1M

Dividing the whole equation \(5y^{2}-20y-20=0\) by \(5\) is legitimate because \(5\ne 0\): \[5y^{2}-20y-20=0 \;\Longleftrightarrow\; y^{2}-4y-4=0.\] Dividing an equation by a non-zero number never changes its roots.

Hint. Smaller coefficients make the next step (sum of roots) much easier and less error-prone.
The reduced equation is \(y^{2}-4y-4=0\).
2The roots are \(p\) and \(q\)1A

Let the \(y\)-coordinates of \(P\) and \(Q\) be \(p\) and \(q\). Since \(P\) and \(Q\) lie on both the line and the circle, \(p\) and \(q\) are exactly the two roots of \((\ast)\).

You can also read this off the diagram: the two green dots on the \(y\)-axis are \(p\) and \(q\).

Hint. We never actually need the numerical values of \(p\) and \(q\) — only their sum. That is the whole trick.
\(p,\,q\) are the roots of \(y^{2}-4y-4=0\).

3 Use the sum of roots

For \(ay^{2}+by+c=0\) with roots \(\alpha,\beta\): \(\displaystyle \alpha+\beta=-\frac{b}{a}\) and \(\alpha\beta=\frac{c}{a}\).

1Apply the sum of roots1M

In \(y^{2}-4y-4=0\) we have \(a=1\), \(b=-4\), \(c=-4\). Therefore \[p+q=-\frac{b}{a}=-\frac{-4}{1}=4.\]

Hint. Watch the double negative: \(-\dfrac{-4}{1}=+4\). Sign slips here are the most common mistake in this question.
\(p+q=4\).
2Mid-point of \(PQ\)1A

The \(y\)-coordinate of the mid-point of \(PQ\) is the average of the \(y\)-coordinates of \(P\) and \(Q\): \[\frac{p+q}{2}=\frac{4}{2}=2.\]

Equivalently: since \(P\) and \(Q\) both lie on the line, the mid-point also lies on the line, and \(y_{\text{mid}}=2\) gives \(x=\dfrac{2+6}{2}=4\), i.e. the mid-point is \((4,2)\).

Hint. The \(x\)-coordinate was never required; the question only asks for the \(y\)-coordinate. Answer: C.
Mid-point of \(PQ = (4,2)\), so the \(y\)-coordinate is \(2\) → Option C.

Alt Alternative: perpendicular from the centre

The perpendicular from the centre \(C(0,4)\) to the chord \(PQ\) bisects \(PQ\). The line \(PQ\) has slope \(2\), so this perpendicular has slope \(-\tfrac12\) and passes through \(C\): \(y-4=-\tfrac12 x\).

Show the perpendicular bisector
Show midpoint \(M\)

Intersecting \(y-4=-\tfrac12 x\) with \(y=2x-6\): \[2x-6-4=-\tfrac12 x \;\Rightarrow\; 4x-20=-x \;\Rightarrow\; x=4,\quad y=2.\] So \(M(4,2)\) and the \(y\)-coordinate is \(2\). Same answer, purely geometric reasoning.

Hint. This method needs one extra fact (centre-to-chord perpendicularity) but avoids the quadratic entirely — a good way to check your algebra.
