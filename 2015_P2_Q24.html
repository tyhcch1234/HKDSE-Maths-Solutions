Topic 12 · Coordinate Geometry — Locus of a Point Equidistant from Two Fixed Points

Locus Perpendicular Bisector Distance Formula Equidistant Condition

Question

The coordinates of the points \(A\) and \(B\) are \((2,\,0)\) and \((1,\,5)\) respectively. If \(P\) is a moving point in the rectangular coordinate plane such that \(P\) is equidistant from \(A\) and \(B\), then the locus of \(P\) is

  1. the perpendicular bisector of \(AB\).
  2. the circle with \(AB\) as a diameter.
  3. the straight line which passes through \(A\) and \(B\).
  4. the angle bisector of \(\angle AOB\), where \(O\) is the origin.

Part (a) — Understanding "Equidistant from two fixed points"

Let us first see the condition \(PA = PB\). Drag the slider to move the moving point \(P\) and watch the two distances.

Equal? —
Equation of locus \(y = \tfrac{x}{5}+\tfrac{11}{5}\)
Step 1

Write down the condition "\(P\) is equidistant from \(A\) and \(B\)" using the distance formula. Let \(P=(x,\,y)\).

\[PA=PB\] \[\sqrt{(x-2)^2+(y-0)^2}=\sqrt{(x-1)^2+(y-5)^2}\]
Hint: "Equidistant" means the two distances are equal. Squaring both sides will remove the square roots — this is the key algebraic move.
Step 2

Square both sides and expand.

\[(x-2)^2+y^2=(x-1)^2+(y-5)^2\] \[x^2-4x+4+y^2=x^2-2x+1+y^2-10y+25\]
Hint: The \(x^2\) and \(y^2\) terms appear on both sides — they cancel out, which is exactly why the locus is a straight line and not a circle. No \(x^2\) or \(y^2\) should survive!
Step 3

Cancel \(x^2\) and \(y^2\), then simplify.

\[-4x+4=-2x+26-10y\] \[10y=2x+22\] \[y=\frac{x}{5}+\frac{11}{5}\]

This is the equation of a straight line — the perpendicular bisector of \(AB\).

Answer: The locus is the perpendicular bisector of \(AB\)  →  Option A.
Hint: A linear equation \(ax+by+c=0\) in the \(xy\)-plane always describes a straight line. To confirm it bisects \(AB\), note it passes through the midpoint \(\left(\tfrac{3}{2},\,\tfrac{5}{2}\right)\) and its slope \(\tfrac{1}{5}\) is the negative reciprocal of the slope of \(AB\).

Step 1 Reasoning

Option A — perpendicular bisector: every point on it is equidistant from \(A\) and \(B\).

Option C — the line \(AB\) itself: take the midpoint \(\left(\tfrac{3}{2},\,\tfrac{5}{2}\right)\); it is equidistant, but take \(A\) itself: then \(PA=0\) while \(PB=AB\neq 0\). So not every point on line \(AB\) works. ✘

Hint: A locus must satisfy the condition at every point on it — not just at some points. Test the easiest points, like \(A\), \(B\), and the midpoint.
Step 2 Reasoning

Option B — circle with \(AB\) as diameter: by Thales' theorem, every point on this circle sees \(AB\) at \(90^\circ\), but the distances \(PA\) and \(PB\) are generally unequal. For example, at the point of the circle farthest from \(A\), \(PA\ne PB\). ✘

Option D — angle bisector of \(\angle AOB\): this line is equidistant from the lines \(OA\) and \(OB\), not from the points \(A\) and \(B\). ✘

Hint: Read the condition carefully: "equidistant from \(A\) and \(B\)" means distance to points, i.e. \(PA=PB\). Option D confuses distance to points with distance to lines.
Conclusion: Only the perpendicular bisector of \(AB\) contains exactly the points equidistant from \(A\) and \(B\).
Step 3

The locus is the perpendicular bisector of the line segment joining the two fixed points.

Final answer: Option A

Summary of Answers

  • Given: \(A=(2,\,0)\), \(B=(1,\,5)\), point \(P\) moves with \(PA=PB\).
  • Key equation: \[(x-2)^2+y^2=(x-1)^2+(y-5)^2 \;\Rightarrow\; y=\tfrac{x}{5}+\tfrac{11}{5}\]
  • Nature of locus: a straight line — the perpendicular bisector of \(AB\).
  • Final answer (MC): Option A — the perpendicular bisector of \(AB\).
  • General principle: The locus of a moving point equidistant from two fixed points is always the perpendicular bisector of the line segment joining the two fixed points.
