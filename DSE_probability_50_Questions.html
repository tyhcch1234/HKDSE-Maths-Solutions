Type 1

Basic Probability, Equally Likely Outcomes and the Complement Rule · 7 questions

Counting favourable and total outcomes; the complement rule

Core idea: when every outcome is equally likely, a probability is just a ratio of counts, \(P(E)=\frac{n(E)}{n(S)}\). The complement rule \(P(E')=1-P(E)\) turns a long "not" or "at least" count into one subtraction, so always ask which of \(E\) and \(E'\) is easier to count.

Two sanity checks: a probability must lie between \(0\) and \(1\), and the probabilities of a complete list of mutually exclusive cases must add to \(1\).

\(P(E)=\frac{n(E)}{n(S)}\)\(P(E')=1-P(E)\)\(0\le P(E)\le 1\)
Q1★☆☆☆2 marks

A bag contains 5 red balls, 3 blue balls and 7 green balls. One ball is drawn at random. Find the probability that the ball drawn is not red.

Answer\(\frac{2}{3}\)
  1. Total number of balls \(=5+3+7=15\), and each ball is equally likely to be drawn.
  2. \(P(\text{red})=\frac{5}{15}=\frac{1}{3}\).
  3. By the complement rule, \(P(\text{not red})=1-\frac{1}{3}=\frac{2}{3}\).
  4. Check by direct counting: the non-red balls number \(3+7=10\), and \(\frac{10}{15}=\frac{2}{3}\). ✓
Common mistake

"Not red" does not mean "blue": it includes blue and green, so there are 10 favourable balls, not 3.

Q2★☆☆☆2 marks

A fair die is rolled once. Find the probability that the number obtained is a prime number.

Answer\(\frac{1}{2}\)
  1. The sample space is \(\{1,2,3,4,5,6\}\), so \(n(S)=6\).
  2. The primes in the sample space are \(2,3,5\), giving 3 favourable outcomes.
  3. \(P(\text{prime})=\frac{3}{6}=\frac{1}{2}\).
Common mistake

1 is not a prime number. Counting \(1,2,3,5\) gives \(\frac{4}{6}=\frac{2}{3}\), which is wrong.

Q3★★☆☆3 marks

A letter is chosen at random from the eleven letters of the word PROBABILITY. Find the probability that the letter chosen is (a) a vowel, (b) a consonant.

Answer(a) \(\frac{4}{11}\)　(b) \(\frac{7}{11}\)
  1. The letters are \(P,R,O,B,A,B,I,L,I,T,Y\): eleven positions, so \(n(S)=11\).
  2. The vowels are \(O,A,I,I\) — four positions (I appears twice and both copies count), so \(P(\text{vowel})=\frac{4}{11}\).
  3. Every letter is either a vowel or a consonant, so \(P(\text{consonant})=1-\frac{4}{11}=\frac{7}{11}\).
  4. Direct check: \(P,R,B,B,L,T,Y\) is seven letters, and \(\frac{7}{11}\) agrees. ✓
Common mistake

Do not shrink the denominator because of the repeated B and I: the eleven positions are equally likely, so \(n(S)=11\). Here Y is treated as a consonant.

Q4★★☆☆3 marks

In a survey of the visitors to a museum, 35% are children and 22% are senior citizens; every other visitor is an adult. A visitor is picked at random. Find the probability that the visitor is an adult.

Answer\(0.43\)
  1. As probabilities: \(P(\text{child})=0.35\) and \(P(\text{senior})=0.22\).
  2. The three groups are mutually exclusive and cover all visitors, so their probabilities add to \(1\).
  3. \(P(\text{adult})=1-(0.35+0.22)=1-0.57=0.43\), i.e. 43% of the visitors.
Common mistake

Subtracting only one of the given groups, for example \(1-0.35=0.65\), forgets the senior citizens.

Q5★★☆☆4 marks

The table shows the favourite sport of each of the 40 students in a class.

SportFootballBasketballBadmintonSwimming
Number of students149710

A student is chosen at random. Find the probability that the student's favourite sport is (a) basketball, (b) football or swimming.

Answer(a) \(\frac{9}{40}\)　(b) \(\frac{3}{5}\)
  1. The frequencies add to \(14+9+7+10=40\), matching the class size, so there are 40 equally likely students.
  2. (a) 9 students choose basketball: \(P(\text{basketball})=\frac{9}{40}\).
  3. (b) The number choosing football or swimming is \(14+10=24\), so \(P=\frac{24}{40}=\frac{3}{5}\).
  4. Check with the complement: badminton or basketball gives \(\frac{7+9}{40}=\frac{16}{40}=\frac{2}{5}\), and \(\frac{3}{5}+\frac{2}{5}=1\). ✓
Common mistake

The total 40 is the denominator, never a numerator: only the two named sports are favourable.

Q6★★★☆5 marks

A square is inscribed in a circle of radius 6 cm, so that the four vertices of the square lie on the circle. A point is chosen at random inside the circle. Find the probability that the point lies inside the square, giving the exact value and a decimal correct to 3 significant figures.

Answer\(\frac{2}{\pi}\approx 0.637\)
  1. Area of the circle \(=\pi r^{2}=\pi(6)^{2}=36\pi\ \text{cm}^{2}\).
  2. The diagonal of the inscribed square is a diameter, so the diagonal is 12 cm. With side \(s\): \(s^{2}+s^{2}=12^{2}\), so \(2s^{2}=144\) and \(s^{2}=72\).
  3. Area of the square \(=s^{2}=72\ \text{cm}^{2}\).
  4. The point is uniformly distributed, so \(P=\frac{72}{36\pi}=\frac{2}{\pi}\).
  5. \(\frac{2}{\pi}=0.6366\ldots\approx 0.637\) (3 s.f.).
Common mistake

The diameter 12 is the square's diagonal, not its side: a side of 12 would give area 144, larger than the circle itself.

Q7★★★☆5 marks

The integers from 1 to 30 inclusive are written on 30 identical cards, one number per card, and one card is drawn at random. Find the probability that the number drawn is (a) a multiple of 6, (b) neither a multiple of 6 nor a multiple of 5.

Answer(a) \(\frac{1}{6}\)　(b) \(\frac{2}{3}\)
  1. There are 30 equally likely cards.
  2. (a) The multiples of 6 are \(6,12,18,24,30\) — five of them, so \(P=\frac{5}{30}=\frac{1}{6}\).
  3. (b) The multiples of 5 are \(5,10,15,20,25,30\) — six of them. Multiples of both 6 and 5 are multiples of 30, namely just 30, so one number is counted twice.
  4. Cards that are a multiple of 6 or of 5: \(5+6-1=10\), so \(30-10=20\) cards are neither.
  5. \(P(\text{neither})=\frac{20}{30}=\frac{2}{3}\).
Common mistake

Forgetting the overlap 30 counts it twice: \(5+6=11\) would leave 19 cards instead of 20.

Type 2

Listing the Sample Space and Counting Outcomes · 7 questions

Systematic listing, tables, and the multiplication principle for totals

Core idea: before computing any probability, write down the whole sample space in a systematic way — a list, a table, or a tree — so that no outcome is counted twice and none is missed. The size of the sample space is found by the multiplication principle: if a choice can be made in \(m\) ways and then another in \(n\) ways, the pair can be formed in \(mn\) ways.

Only when all outcomes are equally likely may you divide counts. "Two dice" always means 36 ordered outcomes, not 21 unordered sums.

\(n(S)=m\times n\)\(P(E)=\frac{n(E)}{n(S)}\)\(\text{two dice}\Rightarrow n(S)=36\)
Q8★☆☆☆2 marks

Two fair coins are tossed. (a) List the sample space. (b) Find the probability of obtaining exactly one head.

Answer(a) \(\{HH,HT,TH,TT\}\)　(b) \(\frac{1}{2}\)
  1. Write the result of the first coin first, then the second: \(HH,\ HT,\ TH,\ TT\), so \(n(S)=4\).
  2. Exactly one head occurs in \(HT\) and \(TH\): \(n(E)=2\).
  3. \(P(\text{exactly one head})=\frac{2}{4}=\frac{1}{2}\).
Common mistake

Listing \(\{HH,HT,TT\}\) merges \(HT\) and \(TH\), which are two different equally likely outcomes, so the denominator changes wrongly.

Q9★☆☆☆2 marks

Two fair dice are thrown. (a) How many outcomes are there in the sample space? (b) Find the probability that the sum of the two numbers is 5.

Answer(a) 36　(b) \(\frac{1}{9}\)
  1. Each die has 6 outcomes, so by the multiplication principle \(n(S)=6\times 6=36\) ordered pairs.
  2. Sums equal to 5: \((1,4),(2,3),(3,2),(4,1)\) — four outcomes.
  3. \(P(\text{sum}=5)=\frac{4}{36}=\frac{1}{9}\).
Common mistake

Taking \(n(S)=21\) by treating \((1,4)\) and \((4,1)\) as the same: the dice are distinguishable, so those are different equally likely outcomes.

Q10★★☆☆3 marks

A fair coin is tossed three times. (a) How many outcomes are in the sample space? (b) Find the probability of obtaining at least two heads.

Answer(a) 8　(b) \(\frac{1}{2}\)
  1. Three tosses give \(2\times 2\times 2=8\) equally likely strings: \(HHH,HHT,HTH,HTT,THH,THT,TTH,TTT\).
  2. "At least two heads" means exactly two heads or three heads: \(HHT,HTH,THH,HHH\) — four outcomes.
  3. \(P=\frac{4}{8}=\frac{1}{2}\).
  4. Alternative: \(P=\frac{\binom{3}{2}+\binom{3}{3}}{2^{3}}=\frac{3+1}{8}=\frac{1}{2}\). ✓
Common mistake

Counting only \(HHT\) and \(HHH\) ignores the order in which the single tail appears — \(HTH\) and \(THH\) are separate outcomes.

Q11★★☆☆3 marks

Three cards are labelled \(A\), \(B\) and \(C\). Two cards are drawn one after another without replacement. (a) List the sample space. (b) Find the probability that the second card drawn is \(B\).

Answer(a) \(\{AB,AC,BA,BC,CA,CB\}\)　(b) \(\frac{1}{3}\)
  1. The first card can be \(A,B\) or \(C\); the second is then one of the remaining two, so \(n(S)=3\times 2=6\).
  2. The ordered outcomes are \(AB,AC,BA,BC,CA,CB\).
  3. The second card is \(B\) in \(AB\) and \(CB\): two outcomes.
  4. \(P(\text{second card is }B)=\frac{2}{6}=\frac{1}{3}\).
  5. Sensible check: by symmetry each of the three cards is equally likely to be second, so the answer must be \(\frac{1}{3}\). ✓
Common mistake

Listing \(AB\) and \(BA\) as one outcome loses the order information that the question asks about, giving \(\frac{2}{3}\) instead of \(\frac{1}{3}\).

Q12★★☆☆4 marks

A restaurant offers a three-course set dinner: a starter, a main course and a dessert. There are 4 starters (one of which is soup), 5 main courses and 3 desserts. A set dinner is chosen at random from all possible combinations. (a) How many different set dinners are possible? (b) Find the probability that the dinner chosen includes soup.

Answer(a) 60　(b) \(\frac{1}{4}\)
  1. By the multiplication principle the number of dinners is \(4\times 5\times 3=60\).
  2. Fix soup as the starter: the main and dessert can still be chosen in \(1\times 5\times 3=15\) ways.
  3. \(P(\text{soup})=\frac{15}{60}=\frac{1}{4}\).
  4. Short cut: exactly one of the 4 starters is soup, so the probability is \(\frac{1}{4}\) whatever the mains and desserts are. ✓
Common mistake

Dividing 15 by 12 or by 4 instead of by 60 — the denominator must be the total number of dinners, not the number of starters.

Q13★★★☆4 marksPast paper · 2022 Paper 2 Q28 ↗

The symbol ⧫ represents an integer from 0 to 9 inclusive, so that \(7\text{⧫}5\) is a three-digit number. All ten values of ⧫ are equally likely. (a) List the ten possible numbers. (b) Find the probability that \(7\text{⧫}5\) is divisible by 3.

Answer(a) 705, 715, …, 795　(b) \(\frac{2}{5}\)
  1. The ten numbers are \(705,715,725,735,745,755,765,775,785,795\), so \(n(S)=10\).
  2. A number is divisible by 3 when its digit sum is a multiple of 3. Here the digit sum is \(7+x+5=12+x\), where \(x\) is the middle digit.
  3. \(12+x\) is a multiple of 3 exactly when \(x\) is a multiple of 3: \(x=0,3,6,9\).
  4. The favourable numbers are \(705,735,765,795\) — four of them, so \(P=\frac{4}{10}=\frac{2}{5}\).
Common mistake

Testing divisibility of the whole number by long division for all ten cases wastes time; the digit-sum rule settles it at once.

Q14★★★☆5 marks

Two-digit numbers are formed by choosing two different digits from \(1,2,3,4,5\), with no repetition. Every such number is equally likely. (a) How many two-digit numbers can be formed? (b) Find the probability that the number is even. (c) Find the probability that the number is even and greater than 40.

Answer(a) 20　(b) \(\frac{2}{5}\)　(c) \(\frac{3}{20}\)
  1. (a) The tens digit has 5 choices and the units digit then 4, so \(5\times 4=20\) numbers.
  2. (b) The number is even exactly when the units digit is 2 or 4. Units digit 2: the tens digit is one of the other 4 digits, giving 4 numbers; similarly 4 numbers end in 4. So \(P(\text{even})=\frac{4+4}{20}=\frac{2}{5}\).
  3. (c) "Even and greater than 40" needs a tens digit of 4 or 5 and an even units digit, but the digits must differ.
  4. Tens digit 5: the units digit may be 2 or 4, giving 2 numbers (52, 54). Tens digit 4: the units digit may only be 2, giving 1 number (42). Total 3.
  5. \(P=\frac{3}{20}=0.15\).
Common mistake

In part (c) the case "tens digit 4, units digit 4" is impossible because digits may not repeat, so only 42 counts — not 42 and 44.

Type 3

Mutually Exclusive Events and the Addition Rule · 7 questions

P(A or B) with and without overlap in the Venn diagram

Core idea: for any two events, \(P(A\cup B)=P(A)+P(B)-P(A\cap B)\). The subtraction removes the outcomes in the overlap, which would otherwise be counted twice. When \(A\) and \(B\) are mutually exclusive the overlap is empty, \(P(A\cap B)=0\), and the rule collapses to \(P(A\cup B)=P(A)+P(B)\).

Two tests decide whether events can be mutually exclusive: they cannot happen together, and therefore \(P(A)+P(B)\le 1\). Also \(P(A'\cap B')=1-P(A\cup B)\) — the region outside both circles.

\(P(A\cup B)=P(A)+P(B)-P(A\cap B)\)\(P(A\cap B)=0\) if mutually exclusive\(P(A'\cap B')=1-P(A\cup B)\)
Q15★☆☆☆2 marks

\(A\) and \(B\) are mutually exclusive events with \(P(A)=0.35\) and \(P(B)=0.4\). Find \(P(A\cup B)\).

Answer\(0.75\)
  1. Mutually exclusive means \(A\) and \(B\) cannot occur together, so \(P(A\cap B)=0\).
  2. \(P(A\cup B)=P(A)+P(B)=0.35+0.4\).
  3. \(=0.75\).
Common mistake

Subtracting an overlap that does not exist, i.e. writing \(0.35+0.4-0=0.75\) but then "correcting" it to \(0.35\times0.4\); the multiplication rule belongs to independent events, not to the addition rule.

Q16★☆☆☆2 marks

\(M\) and \(N\) are two events with \(P(M)=\frac{1}{3}\), \(P(N)=\frac{1}{4}\) and \(P(M\cap N)=\frac{1}{6}\). Find \(P(M\cup N)\).

Answer\(\frac{5}{12}\)
  1. Use the addition rule: \(P(M\cup N)=P(M)+P(N)-P(M\cap N)\).
  2. With denominator 12: \(\frac{4}{12}+\frac{3}{12}-\frac{2}{12}\).
  3. \(=\frac{5}{12}\). Note \(P(M\cap N)\ne 0\), so \(M\) and \(N\) are not mutually exclusive.
Common mistake

Forgetting the \(-\frac{1}{6}\) gives \(\frac{7}{12}\), which double-counts the overlap.

Q17★★☆☆3 marks

A card is drawn at random from 20 cards numbered 1 to 20. Find the probability that the number on the card is even or a multiple of 5.

Answer\(\frac{3}{5}\)
  1. Even numbers: \(2,4,\ldots,20\) — there are 10 of them, so \(P(\text{even})=\frac{10}{20}\).
  2. Multiples of 5: \(5,10,15,20\) — four of them, so \(P(\text{multiple of 5})=\frac{4}{20}\).
  3. Both even and a multiple of 5 means a multiple of 10: \(10,20\) — two cards, \(P=\frac{2}{20}\).
  4. \(P(\text{even or multiple of 5})=\frac{10+4-2}{20}=\frac{12}{20}=\frac{3}{5}\).
Common mistake

Adding \(\frac{10}{20}+\frac{4}{20}\) counts 10 and 20 twice; without the subtraction the answer is \(\frac{7}{10}\), which is too big.

Q18★★☆☆4 marks

In a class of 40 students, 18 study Physics, 15 study Chemistry and 7 study both. A student is chosen at random. (a) Find the probability that the student studies Physics or Chemistry. (b) Find the probability that the student studies neither subject.

Answer(a) \(\frac{13}{20}\)　(b) \(\frac{7}{20}\)
  1. Let \(P\) and \(C\) be the events of studying Physics and Chemistry. Then \(P(P)=\frac{18}{40}\), \(P(C)=\frac{15}{40}\), \(P(P\cap C)=\frac{7}{40}\).
  2. (a) \(P(P\cup C)=\frac{18}{40}+\frac{15}{40}-\frac{7}{40}=\frac{26}{40}=\frac{13}{20}\).
  3. So \(26\) students study at least one of the two subjects.
  4. (b) \(P(\text{neither})=1-\frac{26}{40}=\frac{14}{40}=\frac{7}{20}\), i.e. \(14\) students.
Common mistake

Adding 18 and 15 gives 33 students, which is impossible here: the 7 students taking both would be counted twice, and 33 would leave only 7 taking neither rather than 14.

Q19★★☆☆4 marks

For two events \(A\) and \(B\), \(P(A)=0.5\), \(P(B)=0.4\) and \(P(A\cup B)=0.7\). (a) Find \(P(A\cap B)\). (b) Are \(A\) and \(B\) mutually exclusive? Explain. (c) Find \(P(A'\cap B')\).

Answer(a) \(0.2\)　(b) No, since \(P(A\cap B)\ne 0\)　(c) \(0.3\)
  1. (a) From \(P(A\cup B)=P(A)+P(B)-P(A\cap B)\): \(0.7=0.5+0.4-P(A\cap B)\), so \(P(A\cap B)=0.9-0.7=0.2\).
  2. (b) Mutually exclusive events satisfy \(P(A\cap B)=0\). Here \(P(A\cap B)=0.2\ne 0\), so the events can occur together and are not mutually exclusive.
  3. (c) \(A'\cap B'\) is the region outside both circles, so \(P(A'\cap B')=1-P(A\cup B)=1-0.7=0.3\).
  4. Check: \(0.3+0.2+0.2+0.3=1.0\) when the four regions are \(A\cap B'=0.3\), \(A\cap B=0.2\), \(A'\cap B=0.2\), neither \(=0.3\). ✓
Common mistake

\(P(A'\cap B')\) is not \(P(A')P(B')\); that product rule needs independence. Use \(1-P(A\cup B)\) instead.

Q20★★★☆5 marks

Events \(A\), \(B\) and \(C\) are mutually exclusive and exhaustive, with \(P(A)=0.2\) and \(P(B)=3P(C)\). (a) Find \(P(C)\) and \(P(B)\). (b) Find \(P(A\cup B)\). (c) Find the probability that exactly one of \(A\) and \(C\) occurs.

Answer(a) \(P(C)=0.2\), \(P(B)=0.6\)　(b) \(0.8\)　(c) \(0.4\)
  1. "Mutually exclusive and exhaustive" means the three probabilities add to 1: \(0.2+3P(C)+P(C)=1\).
  2. So \(4P(C)=0.8\), giving \(P(C)=0.2\) and \(P(B)=3(0.2)=0.6\).
  3. (b) \(P(A\cup B)=P(A)+P(B)=0.2+0.6=0.8\), because \(A\) and \(B\) are mutually exclusive.
  4. (c) Since \(A\) and \(C\) cannot occur together, "exactly one of \(A\) and \(C\)" is simply \(A\cup C\), so \(P=0.2+0.2=0.4\).
Common mistake

Writing \(P(B)=3P(C)\) as \(P(B)=0.6\) but then also adding \(P(A\cap B)\ne0\); with mutually exclusive events the overlap is zero and every part is a plain sum.

Q21★★★☆5 marks

One card is drawn at random from a standard pack of 52 playing cards (13 hearts, 13 diamonds, 13 spades, 13 clubs; four kings). (a) Find the probability that the card is a heart or a king. (b) Find the probability that the card is neither a heart nor a king.

Answer(a) \(\frac{4}{13}\)　(b) \(\frac{9}{13}\)
  1. \(P(\text{heart})=\frac{13}{52}\) and \(P(\text{king})=\frac{4}{52}\).
  2. The overlap is the king of hearts: \(P(\text{heart and king})=\frac{1}{52}\).
  3. (a) \(P(\text{heart or king})=\frac{13}{52}+\frac{4}{52}-\frac{1}{52}=\frac{16}{52}=\frac{4}{13}\).
  4. (b) \(P(\text{neither})=1-\frac{16}{52}=\frac{36}{52}=\frac{9}{13}\).
  5. Direct count for (b): \(52-13\ \text{hearts}-3\ \text{other kings}=36\). ✓
Common mistake

Using \(\frac{13+4}{52}=\frac{17}{52}\) counts the king of hearts twice; the correct count is 16.

Type 4

Independent Events and the Multiplication Rule · 7 questions

P(A and B) = P(A)P(B) when one event does not affect the other

Core idea: two events are independent when the occurrence of one gives no information about the other. Then the multiplication rule holds: \(P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B)\). Independence is inherited by the complements, so \(P(A'\cap B')=P(A')P(B')\) as well.

Do not mix the two rules up. "Or" uses the addition rule; "and" uses the multiplication rule; "at least one" is almost always fastest by complement: \(1-P(\text{none})\).

\(P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B)\)\(P(\text{at least one})=1-P(\text{none})\)\(P(A'\cap B')=P(A')P(B')\)
Q22★☆☆☆2 marks

Two lamps work independently. The probability that the first lamp works is 0.9 and the probability that the second lamp works is 0.8. Find the probability that both lamps work.

Answer\(0.72\)
  1. Let \(W_{1}\), \(W_{2}\) be the events that the lamps work; they are independent.
  2. \(P(W_{1}\cap W_{2})=P(W_{1})P(W_{2})=0.9\times0.8\).
  3. \(=0.72\).
Common mistake

Adding instead of multiplying gives \(1.7\), which is not even a probability — a quick reminder that "both" is a multiplication.

Q23★☆☆☆2 marks

A fair coin is tossed and a fair die is rolled. Find the probability of obtaining a head and a 6.

Answer\(\frac{1}{12}\)
  1. The two results are independent, and there are \(2\times6=12\) equally likely combined outcomes.
  2. \(P(\text{head and }6)=P(\text{head})\times P(6)=\frac{1}{2}\times\frac{1}{6}\).
  3. \(=\frac{1}{12}\).
Common mistake

Reporting \(\frac{2}{8}=\frac{1}{4}\) by adding the counts; the sample space has 12 outcomes, not 8.

Q24★★☆☆3 marks

\(A\) and \(B\) are independent events with \(P(A)=0.6\) and \(P(B)=0.3\). Find \(P(A\cup B)\).

Answer\(0.72\)
  1. Independence gives \(P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B)=0.6\times0.3=0.18\).
  2. \(P(A\cup B)=P(A)+P(B)-P(A\cap B)\).
  3. \(=0.6+0.3-0.18=0.72\).
Common mistake

Writing \(P(A\cup B)=0.6+0.3=0.9\) treats the events as mutually exclusive, but independent events with non-zero probabilities can occur together.

Q25★★☆☆4 marks

In a factory, machine \(X\) breaks down on a given day with probability 0.05 and machine \(Y\) with probability 0.08, independently of each other. (a) Find the probability that neither machine breaks down. (b) Find the probability that at least one machine breaks down.

Answer(a) \(0.874\)　(b) \(0.126\)
  1. The complements are independent: \(P(X'\cap Y')=P(X')P(Y')\).
  2. (a) \(P(X')=1-0.05=0.95\) and \(P(Y')=1-0.08=0.92\), so \(P(\text{neither})=0.95\times0.92=0.874\).
  3. (b) \(P(\text{at least one})=1-P(\text{none})=1-0.874=0.126\).
  4. Check the long way: \(0.05(0.92)+0.95(0.08)+0.05(0.08)=0.046+0.076+0.004=0.126\). ✓

Tree Diagrams, Two-Stage Experiments and Conditional Probability · 7 questions

Multiplying along branches, adding across branches, P(B|A)

Core idea: in a two-stage experiment, multiply along a branch and add the branches that give the required event. With replacement the second-stage probabilities stay the same; without replacement the denominator drops by one and the numerator of the colour already taken drops too.

Conditional probability shrinks the sample space to the given event: \(P(B\mid A)=\frac{P(A\cap B)}{P(A)}\), which on a tree means "the branch value divided by the total of all branches that satisfy the condition".

\(P(A\cap B)=P(A)\,P(B\mid A)\)\(P(B\mid A)=\frac{P(A\cap B)}{P(A)}\)\(P(\text{same})=\sum P(\text{both same colour})\)

Type 6

Permutations and Combinations Used in Counting · 7 questions

Order matters or not; restrictions and the gap method

Core idea: if order matters (queues, codes, phone numbers) count permutations, \(P^{n}_{r}=\frac{n!}{(n-r)!}\); if order does not matter (committees, selections) count combinations, \(C^{n}_{r}=\frac{n!}{r!\,(n-r)!}\).

For restrictions, handle the special positions first, then fill the rest. For "not next to each other", arrange the other objects first and place the restricted ones in the gaps they create. When a probability comes from counting, both the numerator and the denominator must count in the same way.

\(P^{n}_{r}=\frac{n!}{(n-r)!}\)\(C^{n}_{r}=\frac{n!}{r!(n-r)!}\)\(\text{gaps}=n+1\) for \(n\) objects in a row

Type 7

Exam-Level Multi-Stage Problems · 8 questions

With and without replacement, "at least one", expected frequency

Core idea: long DSE probability questions are built from the same three moves: multiply along a stage, add the cases that satisfy the event, and switch to the complement when the words are "at least one". Decide at the start whether the experiment is with or without replacement, because that fixes every denominator.

Expected frequency links probability back to data: in \(N\) independent trials, the expected number of successes is \(N\times P(\text{success})\). Marks are usually given for the method (\(C^{n}_{r}\) expressions, a complement, a product of branches) as well as the final value, so write the un-simplified line first.

\(P(\text{at least one})=1-P(\text{none})\)\(\text{expected frequency}=N\,P(E)\)\(P=\frac{C^{a}_{r}C^{b}_{s}}{C^{n}_{r+s}}\)
Q43★☆☆☆2 marks

Reporting 300 or 6: the expected frequency is a count out of 300, so it must be \(300\times\frac{1}{6}\).
