Coordinate Geometry — Circle Equations, Locus & Quadrilaterals

Circle Equation Distance Formula Locus Perpendicular Bisector Perimeter 7 marks

Q The Question 2 + 5 = 7 marks

The circle \(C\) passes through the point \(A(6,11)\) and the centre of \(C\) is the point \(G(0,3)\).

(a) Find the equation of \(C\).  2 marks

(b) \(P\) is a moving point in the rectangular coordinate plane such that \(AP = GP\). Denote the locus of \(P\) by \(\Gamma\).

(i) Find the equation of \(\Gamma\).

(ii) Describe the geometric relationship between \(\Gamma\) and the line segment \(AG\).

(iii) If \(\Gamma\) cuts \(C\) at \(Q\) and \(R\), find the perimeter of the quadrilateral \(AQGR\).  5 marks

a Finding the Equation of Circle \(C\) 2 marks

Drag the slider to rotate the radius \(GA\), and change the centre to see how the radius, and hence the circle, follows.

Horizontal \(GM\) = 6 Vertical \(MA\) = 8 \(r = AG = 10\) Circle: \(x^2+(y-3)^2=100\)
0 / 3
Step 11M
The radius is the distance between the centre \(G(0,3)\) and the point \(A(6,11)\): \[ r = AG = \sqrt{(6-0)^2 + (11-3)^2} = \sqrt{36+64} = \sqrt{100} = 10 \]
Hint: Use the distance formula \(d=\sqrt{(x_2-x_1)^2+(y_2-y_1)^2}\). The vector \(\overrightarrow{GA}=(6,8)\) is a 3-4-5 triangle scaled by 2, so the length is \(2\times 5 = 10\).
Step 21A
A circle with centre \((h,k)\) and radius \(r\) has equation \((x-h)^2+(y-k)^2=r^2\). With centre \((0,3)\) and \(r=10\): \[ \boxed{x^2+(y-3)^2 = 100} \]
Hint: Since the centre lies on the \(y\)-axis (\(h=0\)), the \(x\)-term simplifies to just \(x^2\). Do not expand unless required.
Answer (a): \(x^2+(y-3)^2 = 100\)
Step 3check
Verify: substitute \(A(6,11)\): \[ 6^2 + (11-3)^2 = 36 + 64 = 100 \;\checkmark \]
Hint: Always substitute the given point back into your final equation — it costs seconds and protects your "A" mark.
Confirmed: \(A(6,11)\) lies on \(C\).

b(i) Equation of the Locus \(\Gamma\) 2 marks

Move the point \(P\) along the locus \(\Gamma\) — notice \(AP = GP\) always holds. Drag the slider to slide \(P\).

\(AP\) = — \(GP\) = — \(AP-GP\) = 0.00 \(\Gamma:\;3x+4y-37=0\)
0 / 3
Step 11M
Let \(P\) be \((x,y)\). The condition is \(AP = GP\): \[ \sqrt{(x-6)^2+(y-11)^2} = \sqrt{(x-0)^2+(y-3)^2} \]
Hint: Write both distances with the distance formula first. Squaring will remove the square roots in the next step.
Step 21M
Square both sides and expand: \[ (x-6)^2+(y-11)^2 = x^2+(y-3)^2 \] \[ x^2-12x+36 + y^2-22y+121 = x^2 + y^2-6y+9 \] \[ -12x -22y +157 = -6y + 9 \] \[ -12x -16y + 148 = 0 \] \[ 3x + 4y - 37 = 0 \]
Hint: The \(x^2\) and \(y^2\) terms cancel — that is exactly why the locus of points equidistant from two fixed points is a straight line.
Step 31A
Divide throughout by \(-4\) (or \(4\)) to write the line in its simplest integer form.
Hint: HKDSE accepts any equivalent form, but the standard answer key gives \(3x+4y-37=0\). Keep integer coefficients.
Answer (b)(i): \(\Gamma:\;3x+4y-37=0\)

b(ii) Relationship between \(\Gamma\) and \(AG\) 1 mark

Toggle the perpendicular-bisector construction to see that \(\Gamma\) crosses \(AG\) exactly at its midpoint, at right angles.

Slope \(AG = \tfrac{4}{3}\) Slope \(\Gamma = -\tfrac{3}{4}\) Product \(= -1\) ⇒ perpendicular
0 / 2
Step 11M
Slope of \(AG\): \[ m_{AG} = \frac{11-3}{6-0} = \frac{8}{6} = \frac{4}{3} \] Slope of \(\Gamma\) (from \(3x+4y-37=0\)): \[ y = -\frac{3}{4}x + \frac{37}{4} \quad\Rightarrow\quad m_\Gamma = -\frac{3}{4} \]
Hint: Rearrange the line \(\Gamma\) into slope-intercept form to read off its slope.
Step 21A
Check perpendicularity: \[ m_{AG}\cdot m_\Gamma = \frac{4}{3}\times\left(-\frac{3}{4}\right) = -1 \] Also, \(AG\) midpoint \(=\left(\dfrac{6+0}{2},\dfrac{11+3}{2}\right) = (3,7)\), and substituting into \(\Gamma\): \(3(3)+4(7)-37=0\;\checkmark\)
Hint: A locus of points equidistant from \(A\) and \(G\) is always the perpendicular bisector of \(AG\). The midpoint \(M\) must satisfy \(\Gamma\) because \(M\) is also equidistant from \(A\) and \(G\).
Answer (b)(ii): \(\Gamma\) is the perpendicular bisector of the line segment \(AG\).

b(iii) Perimeter of \(AQGR\) 2 marks

\(\Gamma\) cuts \(C\) at \(Q\) and \(R\). Use the slider to swing the intersection points around and watch \(AQGR\) become a kite / square-tilted rhombus with all four sides equal to 10.

\(AQ\) = 10 \(QG\) = 10 \(GR\) = 10 \(RA\) = 10 Perimeter = 40
0 / 3
Step 11M
Since \(Q\) and \(R\) lie on the circle \(C\) with centre \(G\), we have \[ GQ = GR = 10 \] Since \(Q\) and \(R\) lie on \(\Gamma\), the perpendicular bisector of \(AG\), both are equidistant from \(A\) and \(G\), so \[ AQ = GQ = 10,\qquad AR = GR = 10 \]
Hint: You computed the radius in (a): \(r=10\). Every point on \(C\) is 10 units from \(G\), and every point on \(\Gamma\) is equidistant from \(A\) and \(G\). Together they force all four sides to be 10.
Step 21M
So the quadrilateral \(AQGR\) has all four sides equal to \(10\). Its perimeter is: \[ \text{Perimeter} = AQ + QG + GR + RA = 4 \times 10 = 40 \]
Hint: No need to find the actual coordinates of \(Q\) and \(R\)! The geometry forces every side to be \(10\). This is the key insight of the question.
Step 31A
Numerically: solving \(\Gamma\) and \(C\) together gives \(Q,R\approx(8.68,2.74)\) and \((-1.68,10.51)\), and each side works out to \(10\).
Hint: Substituting \(\Gamma\) into \(C\) produces the intersection points; the distance formula then confirms \(AQ=QG=GR=RA=10\).
Answer (b)(iii): Perimeter of \(AQGR = 40\)

✓ Summary of Answers

  • (a) Radius \(r=AG=10\), so the equation of \(C\) is \(\;x^2+(y-3)^2=100\).
  • (b)(i) The locus \(\Gamma\) is \(\;3x+4y-37=0\).
  • (b)(ii) \(\Gamma\) is the perpendicular bisector of the line segment \(AG\).
  • (b)(iii) Since \(AQ=QG=GR=RA=10\), the perimeter of \(AQGR\) is \(\;4\times 10 = 40\).
