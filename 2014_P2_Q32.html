\(y=b^x\) \(y=c^x\) line \(L\) points \(A,B,C\)

Step 1 — the given graph: \(y=b^x\) lies above \(y=c^x\) for \(x>0\).

HKDSE 2014 Paper 2 · Q32 · Laws of Indices, Exponential & Log Functions

The figure shows the graph of \(y=b^{x}\) and the graph of \(y=c^{x}\) on the same rectangular coordinate system, where \(b\) and \(c\) are positive constants. If a horizontal line \(L\) cuts the \(y\)-axis, the graph of \(y=b^{x}\) and the graph of \(y=c^{x}\) at \(A\), \(B\) and \(C\) respectively, which of the following are true?

I. \(b < c\)
II. \(bc > 1\)
III. \(\dfrac{AB}{AC}=\log_{b}c\)

  • A. I and II only
  • B. I and III only
  • C. II and III only
  • D. I, II and III
STEP 1Read the given information.

Both \(b>0\) and \(c>0\). From the figure, for \(x>0\) the curve \(y=b^{x}\) lies above the curve \(y=c^{x}\); also both curves rise to the right, so they are increasing exponential functions (\(b>1\) and \(c>1\)).

More height at the same \(x\) → bigger base.
Increasing Exponential Function \(k>1\;\Longrightarrow\;y=k^{x} \text{ is increasing}\) Both curves rise to the right, and \(y=b^{x}\) lies above \(y=c^{x}\), so \(b>c>1\).
STEP 2Statement I: test \(b<c\).

Put \(x=1\). Since \(b^{1}\) is above \(c^{1}\) on the graph,

\[ b^{1} > c^{1} \;\Longrightarrow\; b > c. \]

So statement I (\(b<c\)) is false.

Compare the two curves at a single convenient \(x\)-value.
STEP 3Statement II: test \(bc>1\).

Both curves are upward sloping exponential curves with base \(>1\):

\[ b>1 \quad\text{and}\quad c>1. \]

Multiplying two numbers each greater than \(1\):

\[ bc > 1. \]

So statement II is true.

An increasing \(y=k^{x}\) (\(k>0\)) forces \(k>1\).
STEP 4Statement III: set up the horizontal line \(L\).

Let the horizontal line \(L\) be \(y=k\) with \(k>1\). Then \(A=(0,k)\) (on the \(y\)-axis).

\(B\) is on \(y=b^{x}\): \(\;k=b^{x_B}\), so \(x_B=\log_{b}k\), hence

\[ AB = \frac{1}{\log_{b}k}\cdot\log_{b}k \cdot \frac{1}{\log_{b}k} \]

More cleanly: \(AB=x_B=\log_{b}k\).

\(C\) is on \(y=c^{x}\): \(\;k=c^{x_C}\), so \(x_C=\log_{c}k\), hence

\[ AC = x_C = \log_{c}k. \]

A point on \(y=k^{x}\) at height \(k\) is at \(x=\log_{k^{\ast}} k\) — watch the base!
Definition of Logarithm \(k=a^{x}\;\Longleftrightarrow\;x=\log_{a}k\) At the fixed height \(k\), \(B\) gives \(x_{B}=\log_{b}k\) and \(C\) gives \(x_{C}=\log_{c}k\).
STEP 5Simplify the ratio \(\dfrac{AB}{AC}\).

\[ \frac{AB}{AC}=\frac{\log_{b}k}{\log_{c}k} =\frac{1/\log_{k}b}{1/\log_{k}c} =\frac{\log_{k}c}{\log_{k}b} =\log_{b}c. \]

So statement III is true.

Change of base, then cancel \(\log_{k}\) terms.
Change of Base Formula \(\log_{b}k=\dfrac{1}{\log_{k}b}\) Rewriting both logs with base \(k\) lets the \(\log_{k}\) factors cancel, giving \(\dfrac{AB}{AC}=\log_{b}c\).
ANSWER  Only II and III are true  \(\Rightarrow\)  Option C.

Question. The figure shows the graphs of \(y=b^{x}\) and \(y=c^{x}\), where \(c>b>1\). A horizontal line \(L:y=k\ (k>1)\) cuts the \(y\)-axis, \(y=b^{x}\) and \(y=c^{x}\) at \(A\), \(B\) and \(C\) respectively. (a) Show that \(\dfrac{AB}{AC}=\log_{b}c\). (b) Find \(\dfrac{AB}{AC}\) when \(b=2\) and \(c=8\).

Show the worked solution
Step 1. \(A=(0,\ k)\). Since \(k=b^{x_{B}}\), \(x_{B}=\log_{b}k\), so \(AB=\log_{b}k\).
Step 2. Since \(k=c^{x_{C}}\), \(x_{C}=\log_{c}k\), so \(AC=\log_{c}k\).
Step 3. \(\dfrac{AB}{AC}=\dfrac{\log_{b}k}{\log_{c}k}=\dfrac{1/\log_{k}b}{1/\log_{k}c}=\dfrac{\log_{k}c}{\log_{k}b}=\log_{b}c\).
Step 4. With \(b=2\) and \(c=8\): \(\dfrac{AB}{AC}=\log_{2}8=3\).
Answer: (a) \(\log_{b}c\)   (b) \(3\)
