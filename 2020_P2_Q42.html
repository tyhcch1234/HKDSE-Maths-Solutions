Counting Queues with a "No Two Boys Adjacent" Condition

Topic 14 · Probability & Permutations

Permutations Arrangements Gap Method Multiplication Principle

Question

A queue is formed by 6 boys and 5 girls. If no boys are next to each other, how many different queues can be formed?
A86 400
B172 800
C213 444
D39 916 800

Core Concept — The "Gap Method"

When a condition says "no two of these items may be adjacent", the fastest strategy is:

① Arrange the other items first (here: the girls) — this creates fixed gaps.
② Drop the restricted items (boys) into those gaps — at most one per gap.

With 5 girls arranged in a row there are \(5 + 1 = \mathbf{6}\) gaps: one before the first girl, four between consecutive girls, and one after the last girl. Since we have exactly 6 boys and 6 gaps, every gap must receive exactly one boy.

The queue contains 6 boys and 5 girls, total 11 people. The restriction is:

  • No two boys may stand next to each other.

Girls have no restriction — they may be neighbours.

Hint: "No boys are next to each other" is not "boys and girls alternate". It only forbids B–B pairs. Girls may stand beside each other if there are enough gaps elsewhere.

Trying to arrange all 11 people at once and then "subtract forbidden cases" is messy. Instead we use the classic Gap Method:

  • First arrange the unrestricted group (the girls).
  • Then insert the restricted group (the boys) into the spaces created.
Why girls first? Because with 5 girls and 6 boys, the girls create exactly 6 gaps — just enough. If we placed boys first, we couldn't guarantee separation as easily.

The 5 girls can be arranged in a row in:

\[ P_5^5 = 5! = 120 \]

ways. Call this the first factor of our answer.

Formula: \(P_n^n = n!\) — arranging \(n\) distinct objects in \(n\) positions. Here \(n=5\), so \(5! = 5\times4\times3\times2\times1 = 120\).

After placing the girls in a row:

\[ \_\ G\ \_\ G\ \_\ G\ \_\ G\ \_\ G\ \_ \]

We count the spaces between (and outside) the girls:

  • 1 gap before the first girl
  • 4 gaps between the 5 girls
  • 1 gap after the last girl

Total: \(1 + 4 + 1 = \mathbf{6}\) gaps.

Key pattern: \(n\) items in a row always create \(n+1\) gaps. With \(n = 5\) girls, we get \(5+1 = 6\) gaps — exactly matching the 6 boys!

To keep boys apart, each gap can hold at most one boy. We have 6 boys and 6 gaps, so every gap must contain exactly one boy.

The number of ways to assign 6 distinct boys to 6 distinct gaps is:

\[ P_6^6 = 6! = 720 \]
Formula: \(P_n^r = \dfrac{n!}{(n-r)!}\). With \(n=r=6\), this simplifies to \(6! = 720\). Since boys are distinct people, order matters.

The two tasks are independent — arranging the girls does not affect which gaps exist, and choosing the boy order in gaps does not affect the girls. Multiply the counts:

\[ P_5^5 \times P_6^6 = 5! \times 6! \]
\[ = 120 \times 720 \]
\[ = 86\,400 \]

So the required number of queues is 86 400.

Compare 86 400 with the options:

  • A. 86 400 ✔ matches our calculation
  • B. 172 800 — this is \(5! \times 6! \times 2\), an over-count
  • C. 213 444 — not of factorial form
  • D. 39 916 800 — this is \(11!/(6!\,5!)\), a total-split miscount

The answer is A.

Sanity check: Without any restriction there are \(11! \approx 3.99\times10^{7}\) arrangements. Our restricted count (86 400) is much smaller — which is expected since most arrangements have boys adjacent.
