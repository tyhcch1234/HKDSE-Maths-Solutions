Geometry — Congruent & Similar Triangles in a Quadrilateral

Congruence (AAS) Isosceles Triangle Similar Triangles Diagonals Proof & Counting

QUESTION The Full Problem

In Figure 1, \(ABCD\) is a quadrilateral. The diagonals \(AC\) and \(BD\) intersect at \(E\). It is given that \(BE = CE\) and \(\angle BAC = \angle BDC\).

(a) Prove that \(\triangle ABC \cong \triangle DCB\). (2 marks)

(b) Consider the triangles in Figure 1.

(i) How many pairs of congruent triangles are there?

(ii) How many pairs of similar triangles are there? (4 marks)

PART (a) Prove \(\triangle ABC \cong \triangle DCB\)

Move the sliders to drag the vertices \(A\) and \(D\). Notice that \(BE=CE\) always stays true when \(\angle BAC=\angle BDC\) — this is the key structural fact the proof exploits.

Step 11M

Since \(BE = CE\), triangle \(BEC\) is an isosceles triangle with vertex \(E\). Therefore its base angles at \(B\) and \(C\) are equal:

\[ \angle BCE = \angle CBE \quad (\text{base } \angle\text{s, isos. } \triangle) \]
Hint: In \(\triangle BEC\), the equal sides \(BE\) and \(CE\) meet at \(E\), so the base is \(BC\) and the base angles are \(\angle BCE\) and \(\angle CBE\).
Step 21M

The question gives us a second pair of equal angles:

\[ \angle BAC = \angle BDC \quad (\text{given}) \]

And \(BC\) is a side shared by both triangles:

\[ BC = BC \quad (\text{common side}) \]
Hint: We now have two equal angle-pairs with a corresponding non-included side equal — exactly the AAS condition.
Step 31A

Matching corresponding vertices carefully — \(B \leftrightarrow C\), \(C \leftrightarrow B\), \(A \leftrightarrow D\):

\[ \left.\begin{aligned}\angle BCE &= \angle CBE\\ \angle BAC &= \angle BDC\\ BC &= BC\end{aligned}\right\}\Rightarrow \triangle ABC \cong \triangle DCB \quad (\text{AAS}) \]
✔ \(\triangle ABC \cong \triangle DCB\) (AAS)
Note on naming: Write the vertices in the correct order — \(A\to D\), \(B\to C\), \(C\to B\) — so that corresponding angles and sides line up. The marking scheme awards 2 marks for a full proof with reasons, 1 mark if reasons are omitted.

PART (b)(i) Number of Pairs of Congruent Triangles

Toggle each candidate pair below and watch the two triangles being compared. A pair is congruent only if one can be rigidly moved onto the other (same shape and same size).

Showing 1 pair
Step 11M

From part (a) we already have one congruent pair:

\[ \triangle ABC \cong \triangle DCB \]
Hint: This pair uses the whole quadrilateral's two "big" triangles cut by diagonal \(BC\).
Step 21M

Using \(\triangle ABC \cong \triangle DCB\), corresponding parts are equal:

\[ AB = DC, \quad AC = DB, \quad \angle ABE = \angle DCE,\ \angle BAE = \angle CDE \]

Subtracting the shared segment from equal diagonals: since \(AC = DB\) and \(BE = CE\) (given),

\[ AE = AC - CE = DB - BE = DE \]

Also \(BE = CE\) (given) and \(\angle AEB = \angle DEC\) (vertically opposite angles). Hence

\[ \triangle ABE \cong \triangle DCE \quad (\text{SAS}) \]
Hint: The vertically opposite angles at \(E\) give the included-angle condition.
Step 31M

Similarly, \(AE = DE\), \(BE = CE\) together with \(\angle AED = \angle DEA\) gives the third pair:

\[ \triangle ADE \cong \triangle DAE \quad (\text{SAS}) \]

Together with the pairs found above, and noting the overlapping case, the three distinct congruent pairs are:

✔ There are 3 pairs of congruent triangles. (1A)
Careful: Watch for pairs that are the same set of triangles written in different order — those count as one pair only.

PART (b)(ii) Number of Pairs of Similar Triangles

Similar triangles have equal angles but may be scaled. Drag the slider to scale one triangle on top of another — the shape is preserved, showing similarity.

scale = 0.60
Step 11M

Because \(ABCD\) is shown to have \(\angle BAC = \angle BDC\) and equal base angles, the figure contains several triangles sharing angles at \(E\):

\[ \angle AEB = \angle DEC \ (\text{vert. opp. } \angle\text{s}), \qquad \angle BAE = \angle CDE \] \[ \Rightarrow \triangle ABE \sim \triangle DCE \quad (\text{AAA}) \]
Hint: Vertically opposite angles give one equal pair; the angles from the quadrilateral give another.
Step 21M

Working around the figure with the same equal-angle chase:

\[ \triangle ADE \sim \triangle BCE \quad (\text{AAA}) \]

And the two big triangles cut by the other diagonal also match in shape:

\[ \triangle ABC \sim \triangle DCB \quad (\text{AAA}) \]
Hint: Every triangle inside the figure can be matched to a partner via one of these three angle relationships.
Step 31A

Listing all distinct similar pairs found by the angle chase:

  • \(\triangle ABE \sim \triangle DCE\)
  • \(\triangle ADE \sim \triangle BCE\)
  • \(\triangle ABC \sim \triangle DCB\)
  • \(\triangle ABD \sim \triangle DCA\)
✔ There are 4 pairs of similar triangles. (1A)
Why more similar than congruent? Similarity only needs equal angles, so scaled copies also qualify — giving more pairs than congruence.

  • (b)(i) There are 3 pairs of congruent triangles. [1]
  • (b)(ii) There are 4 pairs of similar triangles. [1]
