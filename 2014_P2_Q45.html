Statistics — How a linear change of every datum affects the variance

Variance Linear Transformation Spread of Data Multiple-Choice

Question 2014MC#45

If the variance of the four numbers \(a\), \(b\), \(c\) and \(d\) is \(9\), then the variance of the four numbers \(14-a\), \(14-b\), \(14-c\) and \(14-d\) is

A.  5
B.  9
C.  23
D.  121

Key idea: adding a constant shifts every datum equally (spread unchanged), while multiplying every datum by a constant \(k\) multiplies the variance by \(k^{2}\).

Part (a) Think  Break the transformation into two moves

The new numbers \(14-a,\;14-b,\;14-c,\;14-d\) are obtained from the old numbers \(a,b,c,d\) in two separate stages:

Stage 1 — Multiply by \(-1\): \(\;a,\;b,\;c,\;d \;\longrightarrow\; -a,\;-b,\;-c,\;-d\)
Stage 2 — Add \(14\): \(\;-a,\;-b,\;-c,\;-d \;\longrightarrow\; 14-a,\;14-b,\;14-c,\;14-d\)

1 Effect of multiplying every datum by \(-1\)

If every datum is multiplied by a constant \(k\), the variance is multiplied by \(k^{2}\).

\[ \operatorname{Var}(kX) = k^{2}\operatorname{Var}(X) \]

Here \(k=-1\), so

\[ \operatorname{Var}(-a,-b,-c,-d) = (-1)^{2}\times \operatorname{Var}(a,b,c,d) = 1 \times 9 = 9 \]

Why \(k^{2}\) and not \(k\)? Variance measures squared deviation. Changing sign reverses direction but the distance from the mean is unchanged; squaring removes the sign.

After multiplying by \(-1\), the variance is still \(9\).
2 Effect of adding \(14\) to every datum

Adding a constant \(c\) to every datum shifts the whole set but keeps the deviations identical:

\[ \operatorname{Var}(X+c) = \operatorname{Var}(X) \]

Because the mean also increases by \(14\), each deviation \((x_i + 14) - (\bar x + 14) = x_i - \bar x\) is unchanged.

Everyday picture: moving a whole classroom of students \(14\) metres to the right does not change how spread out they are.

After adding \(14\), the variance is still \(9\).
3 Combine and conclude

Applying both stages:

\[ \operatorname{Var}(14-a,\dots,14-d) = \operatorname{Var}\bigl((-1)(a,\dots,d) + 14\bigr) = (-1)^{2}\operatorname{Var}(a,\dots,d) = 9 \]

Watch out: add \(14\) inside doesn't matter — \(14-a = -a+14\), the order of the two operations is irrelevant to the result.

The required variance is \(9\) → Answer: B

1 Check the mean of the new data

Let the original mean be \(\bar x = \dfrac{a+b+c+d}{4}\). The new numbers are \(14-a,14-b,14-c,14-d\), so

\[ \bar x_{\text{new}} = \frac{(14-a)+(14-b)+(14-c)+(14-d)}{4} = \frac{56 - (a+b+c+d)}{4} = 14 - \bar x \]

Observation: the new mean is the "mirror" of the old mean about \(7\). The centre moves, but the spread does not.

New mean \(=14-\bar x\).
2 Compare each deviation

For each datum, the new deviation from the new mean is

\[ (14-a) - (14-\bar x) = -(a-\bar x) \]

Squaring removes the negative sign:

\[ (a-\bar x)^{2} \;\longrightarrow\; \bigl(-(a-\bar x)\bigr)^{2} = (a-\bar x)^{2} \]

So every single squared deviation is identical — only the order of the numbers along the number line is reversed (mirror image about \(7\)).

Each squared deviation is unchanged.
3 Sum of squared deviations

Since every squared deviation is the same as before, the total is the same:

\[ \sum_{i}\bigl(x_i^{\text{new}}-\bar x_{\text{new}}\bigr)^{2} = \sum_{i}(x_i-\bar x)^{2} \]

Dividing by \(n=4\) gives

\[ \operatorname{Var} = \frac{\sum (x_i-\bar x)^{2}}{4} = 9 \]

General rule: \(\operatorname{Var}(mX+c) = m^{2}\operatorname{Var}(X)\). Here \(m=-1,\;c=14\), so the multiplier is \((-1)^2 = 1\).

Therefore the new variance is \(9\).  →  Option B

Summary — Answers at a glance

  • Core rule 1 (shift): \(\operatorname{Var}(X+c) = \operatorname{Var}(X)\) — adding a constant never changes the spread.
  • Core rule 2 (scale): \(\operatorname{Var}(kX) = k^{2}\operatorname{Var}(X)\) — multiplying by \(k\) multiplies the variance by \(k^{2}\).
  • Apply to this question: \(14-a = (-1)\cdot a + 14\), so the multiplier is \(k = -1\).
  • Computation: \(\operatorname{Var} = (-1)^{2} \times 9 = 1 \times 9 = 9\).
  • Why not 121? \(121 = 11^{2}\) would require multiplying the data by \(11\), but here we only multiply by \(-1\).
  • Why not 23? \(23 = 9+14\) wrongly adds the shift \(14\) into the variance — shifts are ignored.
Final answer: \(\operatorname{Var}(14-a,14-b,14-c,14-d) = 9\)  ➜  B
