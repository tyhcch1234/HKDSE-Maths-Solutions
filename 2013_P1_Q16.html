2013 Paper 1 — Question 16

Topic: Probability (Combination & Complementary Events) · 4 marks

Probability Combination \(C^n_r\) "At least" — case split Complementary event \(1-P\)

QQuestion

A box contains 5 white cups and 11 blue cups. If 6 cups are randomly drawn from the box at the same time,

(a) find the probability that at least 4 white cups are drawn; [2 marks]
(b) find the probability that at least 3 blue cups are drawn; [2 marks]

👀See the box

Total 5 white + 11 blue = 16 cups · We draw 6 at the same time.

💡Key Ideas Before We Start

Core Formula
The number of ways to choose \(r\) cups from \(n\) cups is \[ C^{n}_{r} = \frac{n!}{r!\,(n-r)!} \] Since cups of the same colour are indistinguishable for counting, we count combinations, not arrangements.
Total Sample Space
Choosing any 6 cups out of 16: \[ C^{16}_{6} = 8008 \] This number will appear as the denominator in both parts.
"At least" Strategy
"At least \(k\)" means \(k, k+1, k+2, \dots\) — so we split into cases and add.
But first check: is the complement shorter? Part (b) will show you how powerful \(P = 1 - P(\text{complement})\) can be.

aPart (a): \(P(\text{at least 4 white cups})\)

Click each step to reveal the working. Try to predict the next line first!

1 Identify the total number of outcomes ▼

We draw 6 cups out of 16 total. Order does not matter.

\[ \text{Total} = C^{16}_{6} = \frac{16!}{6!\,10!} = 8008 \]
Hint
This 8008 is the denominator for every probability in this question.
2 Break "at least 4 white" into cases ▼

We draw 6 cups in total. If \(w\) = number of white cups, then:

at least 4 white  ⟺  \(w = 4\) or \(w = 5\) or \(w = 6\)

Note: \(w = 6\) is possible because there are 5 white cups? No — wait! Let's check the maximum.

Careful!
There are only 5 white cups in the box, so we can never draw 6 white cups. Therefore the valid cases are \(w = 4\) and \(w = 5\) only.
3 Count the ways for each valid case ▼

For each case: choose the white cups from 5 white, and the remaining cups from 11 blue.

CaseWhite chosenBlue chosenNumber of ways
\(w = 4\) \(C^{5}_{4} = 5\) \(C^{11}_{2} = 55\) \(5 \times 55 = 275\)
\(w = 5\) \(C^{5}_{5} = 1\) \(C^{11}_{1} = 11\) \(1 \times 11 = 11\)
\(w = 6\) impossible — not possible
Hint — why multiply?
Choosing the white cups and choosing the blue cups are independent tasks performed together, so we use the multiplication principle. Adding the cases uses the addition principle (mutually exclusive cases).
4 Form the probability and simplify ▼
\[ P(\text{at least 4 white}) = \frac{C^{5}_{4}C^{11}_{2} + C^{5}_{5}C^{11}_{1}}{C^{16}_{6}} \] \[ = \frac{275 + 11}{8008} = \frac{286}{8008} = \frac{1}{28} \]
✅ Answer (a): \(\dfrac{1}{28}\)
Simplify tip
Divide numerator and denominator by 286: \(286 \div 286 = 1\) and \(8008 \div 286 = 28\).

bPart (b): \(P(\text{at least 3 blue cups})\)

This part rewards a clever shortcut. Let's find it together.

1 Try the direct case-split first (and see the pain) ▼

If \(b\) = number of blue cups, "at least 3 blue" means:

\(b = 3,\ 4,\ 5,\ 6\)

That's four separate cases to count and add — long, and easy to make errors under exam pressure.

Pause & think
Is there a shorter route? Look at the complement: what is the opposite of "at least 3 blue"?
2 Use the complementary event ▼

The complement of "at least 3 blue cups" is:

"fewer than 3 blue cups"  ⟺  \(b \le 2\)

\[ P(\text{at least 3 blue}) = 1 - P(\text{at most 2 blue}) \]
Key formula
For any event \(E\), \(P(E) = 1 - P(E')\), where \(E'\\) is the complement of \(E\).
3 Spot the shortcut: "at most 2 blue" ⟺ "at least 4 white" ▼

6 cups in total. If at most 2 are blue, then at least \(6-2 = 4\) must be white.

So "at most 2 blue" is exactly the same event as "at least 4 white" — which is precisely what we found in part (a)!

Beautiful
We don't need a single new calculation. We simply reuse: \[ P(\text{at most 2 blue}) = P(\text{at least 4 white}) = \frac{1}{28} \]
3.57%
The rest — 96.43%
at most 2 blue = \(1/28 \approx 3.57\%\) at least 3 blue = \(27/28 \approx 96.43\%\)
4 Compute the final answer ▼
\[ P(\text{at least 3 blue}) = 1 - \frac{1}{28} = \frac{28-1}{28} = \frac{27}{28} \]
✅ Answer (b): \(\dfrac{27}{28}\)
Exam insight
Always scan for the complement. If "at least \(k\)" would need many cases, "at most \(k-1\)" might need only a few — or, as here, be a case you have already solved.
