Question

The equation of the parabola $\Gamma$ is $y = 2x^2 - 2kx + 2x - 3k + 8$, where $k$ is a real constant. Denote the straight line $y = 19$ by $L$.

(a) Prove that $L$ and $\Gamma$ intersect at two distinct points. [3 marks]

(b) The points of intersection of $L$ and $\Gamma$ are $A$ and $B$.
(i) Let $a$ and $b$ be the $x$-coordinates of $A$ and $B$ respectively. Prove that $(a-b)^2 = k^2 + 4k + 23$.
(ii) Is it possible that the distance between $A$ and $B$ is less than $4$? Explain your answer. [5 marks]

1(a) Step 1 — Form the intersection equation

Put $y = 19$ into the equation of $\Gamma$:

$$2x^2 - 2kx + 2x - 3k + 8 = 19$$

Rearranging,

$$2x^2 + (2 - 2k)x - (3k + 11) = 0.$$

To find intersections, solve the parabola and the line simultaneously.
2(a) Step 2 — Compute the discriminant

For $2x^2 + (2-2k)x - (3k+11) = 0$,

$$ \begin{aligned} \Delta &= (2-2k)^2 - 4(2)\bigl[-(3k+11)\bigr]\\ &= 4k^2 + 16k + 92\\ &= 4(k^2 + 4k + 2^2 - 2^2) + 92\\ &= 4(k+2)^2 + 76. \end{aligned}$$

1M each for forming the equation and simplifying $\Delta$.

Complete the square inside the bracket so that $\Delta$ becomes clearly positive.
3(a) Conclusion

Since $4(k+2)^2 \ge 0$, we have $\Delta = 4(k+2)^2 + 76 > 0$ for every real $k$.

Therefore $L$ and $\Gamma$ intersect at two distinct points. 1

A positive discriminant ⇔ two distinct real roots ⇔ two distinct intersection points.
4(b)(i) Step 1 — Sum and product of roots

From (a), the roots of $2x^2 + (2-2k)x - (3k+11) = 0$ are exactly $a$ and $b$ (the $x$-coordinates of $A$ and $B$), so

$$a + b = \frac{-(2-2k)}{2} = k - 1, \qquad ab = \frac{-(3k+11)}{2}.$$

Apply the sum-of-roots and product-of-roots formulas. 1M
5(b)(i) Step 2 — Prove the identity

$$ \begin{aligned} (a-b)^2 &= a^2 - 2ab + b^2\\ &= (a+b)^2 - 4ab\\ &= (k-1)^2 - 4\left(\frac{-(3k+11)}{2}\right)\\ &= k^2 - 2k + 1 + 6k + 22\\ &= k^2 + 4k + 23. \end{aligned}$$

Hence $(a-b)^2 = k^2 + 4k + 23$. 1

Use the factorisation $(a-b)^2 = (a+b)^2 - 4ab$ to avoid finding $a$ and $b$ separately.
6(b)(ii) Step 1 — Set up the inequality

Since $A$ and $B$ both lie on $y = 19$, the distance between them is $AB = |a-b|$.

Suppose $AB < 4$; then

$$\sqrt{(a-b)^2} < 4 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad (a-b)^2 < 16.$$

A and B share the same $y$-coordinate, so the horizontal gap is the whole distance. 1M
7(b)(ii) Step 2 — Discriminant test

Using (b)(i):

$$k^2 + 4k + 23 < 16 \;\Longrightarrow\; k^2 + 4k + 7 < 0.$$

Now the discriminant of $k^2 + 4k + 7$ is

$$4^2 - 4(1)(7) = -12 < 0,$$

and the coefficient of $k^2$ is positive, so $k^2 + 4k + 7 > 0$ for all real $k$.

A positive quadratic with negative discriminant is always positive — it never dips below zero.
8(b)(ii) Conclusion

Hence $k^2 + 4k + 7 < 0$ has no real solution, i.e. the assumption $AB < 4$ leads to a contradiction.

So it is not possible for the distance between $A$ and $B$ to be less than $4$. 1A

Indeed $AB^2 = (k+2)^2 + 19 \ge 19$, so $AB \ge \sqrt{19} \approx 4.36 > 4$.

The minimum of $k^2+4k+23$ is $19$ (at $k = -2$), so $AB$ has minimum value $\sqrt{19} \approx 4.36$.
Answer (a) $\Delta = 4(k+2)^2 + 76 > 0$, so $L$ and $\Gamma$ meet at two distinct points.
(b)(i) $(a-b)^2 = k^2 + 4k + 23$.   (b)(ii) No — $AB^2 = (k+2)^2 + 19 \ge 19$, so $AB \ge \sqrt{19} > 4$.
